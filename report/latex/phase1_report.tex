\begin{abstract}
ML-DSA (FIPS~204) is the lattice based digital signature that NIST standardised. It was designed for a single signer.
Splitting its key among several parties is hard, because rejection sampling and rounding do not fit well with secret
sharing. NIST's 2026 First Call for multi party threshold schemes (IR~8214C) has four proposals whose signatures pass an
unchanged ML-DSA verifier. They are Mithril, Quorus, SplitForge/Trilithium and TALUS. We survey 33 sources on these schemes
and on leakage attacks against lattice signatures. Our core problem is the TALUS signing cap, the limit on how many
signatures one key may produce. In Phase~2 we built a leakage testing harness. It has an ML-DSA implementation that passes
81 NIST ACVP test vectors, a view of what each scheme releases, and estimators based on least squares, bounded noise and
integer programming. In our model of the TALUS $s_2$ channel it recovers the whole secret of ML-DSA-44 in 18 of 20 trials
at $7\times10^4$ signatures ($2^{16.1}$). For 95\% success it needs about 3.1 bits more than the cap allows for ML-DSA-44
and 3.5 bits more for ML-DSA-65 and ML-DSA-87. That is about 1 bit above the authors' uniqueness wall. The margin is thin,
but it is not a break. As controls, the estimator recovers the secret in 9 of 9 runs on a leaky variant and returns no
false key for 18 genuine ML-DSA keys. Along the way we reproduce both published attacks on an early TALUS version. We find
a gap between Mithril's parameters and its proof, although its real Go transcripts show no $s_2$ leak. We also show that a
predecessor design gives away the key from one signature at full ML-DSA size. These last two findings await coordinated
disclosure.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\textbf{Digital signatures and the quantum threat.} A digital signature lets a signer prove that a message came from them
and was not changed. A large quantum computer would break RSA and ECDSA, so NIST standardised post quantum replacements.
The main one is ML-DSA, specified in FIPS~204~\cite{fips204} and derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium~\cite{dilithium}. Its
security rests on two lattice problems, Module-LWE (learning with errors) and Module-SIS.

\textbf{How ML-DSA signs.} In simple terms, the signer hides its secret inside a random looking answer, and throws the
answer away whenever it might give the secret away. In more detail, all arithmetic is in the ring
$R_q=\mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^n+1)$. Its elements are polynomials of degree below $n=256$ with coefficients modulo the prime
$q=8380417$. $A$ is a public $k\times\ell$ matrix of such polynomials. The secret key is a pair of short vectors
$(s_1,s_2)$ with coefficients in $[-\eta,\eta]$. The public key is $t=As_1+s_2$, and only its high part $t_1$ is published.
We write $t=t_12^d+t_0$, where $t_0$ holds the $d=13$ dropped low bits. To sign, the signer picks a random nonce $y$ with
coefficients below $\gamma_1$. It computes $w=Ay$ and the high bits $w_1=\HB(w)$. It then derives a challenge
$c=H(\mathit{msg},w_1)$. This is a polynomial with exactly $\tau$ coefficients equal to $\pm1$ and the rest zero. The
output is $z=y+cs_1$ together with a short hint $h$. We write $\norm{x}$ for the largest absolute coefficient of $x$.
The number $\beta=\tau\eta$ bounds $\norm{cs}$ for any secret $s$. Two rejection checks follow. The first,
$\norm{z}<\gamma_1-\beta$, hides $s_1$. The second, $\norm{\LB(w-cs_2)}<\gamma_2-\beta$, hides $s_2$. Here $\gamma_2$ is
the rounding range of $\LB$. If either check fails, the signer starts again with a fresh $y$. This design is called
Fiat--Shamir with aborts. It makes accepted signatures statistically independent of the key. Table~\ref{tab:params} lists
the parameter values.

\textbf{Why rejection keeps signatures safe.} Think of $z$ as a point in a box. The nonce $y$ is uniform on a box of half
width $\gamma_1$, and $cs_1$ moves it by at most $\beta$. So every point inside the smaller box of half width
$\gamma_1-\beta$ is equally likely, whatever $s_1$ was used. Accepting only those points therefore hides $s_1$ completely.
The challenge $c$ has only $\tau$ nonzero entries. So if anything does leak, the attacker gets one noisy linear equation
with small coefficients per signature. All the sample counts in this report come from this fact. Any threshold design that
changes what is accepted, what is rejected or what else is published must bring back this independence. This is the
property we test.

\textbf{Why a threshold version is hard.} In a $(T,N)$ threshold scheme, any $T$ of the $N$ parties can sign together,
and fewer than $T$ learn nothing. Shamir secret sharing~\cite{shamir} hides a secret as the constant term of a random
polynomial of degree $T-1$. Feldman~\cite{feldman} adds commitments $g^{a_i}$ to its coefficients. Threshold Schnorr
schemes such as FROST~\cite{frost} simply add up partial signatures. ML-DSA loses all three of these conveniences. Shares
of a short secret are not short. $\HB$ is not additive, so parties cannot round their own pieces of $w$. The $s_2$ check
needs the full secret. And the lattice version of a Feldman commitment, $Ax$, does not hide $x$
(Section~\ref{sec:attacks}). Two facts drive most attacks below. First, $A$ is tall ($k\ge\ell$), so anyone who sees $Ax$
can recover $x$ by Gaussian elimination. Second, $z=y+cs_1$ holds over the integers, so any leak of the nonce $y$ gives
$s_1=c^{-1}(z-y)$.

\textbf{Why now, and what this report adds.} NIST's First Call (IR~8214C)~\cite{ir8214c} expects early packages from late
2026 and full packages from March 2027, so outside checking is most useful now. This year has already shown why. In July
2026 Niot~\cite{niot} recovered keys from earlier TALUS versions. TALUS v0.22~\cite{taluspw} answered with a limit on the
number of signatures per key, and nobody had measured its safety margin independently. After the Phase~1 review, that
signing cap is our core problem (P1). The Mithril, rejected attempt and Kao questions (P2 to P4) are stretch goals. The
report gives four things. First, a survey of the four proposals, the attack toolbox and recent work. Second, the problem
statement. Third, our proposed solution, a leakage testing harness (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). Its main estimator uses the hard
edges of the TALUS noise and finishes with integer programming. Fourth, simulation results with trial counts and seeds. They
show that our method needs about $5\times10^4$ times fewer signatures than the least squares baseline, yet still stays
about 3 bits above the cap.

\textbf{How we found the sources.} We started from the First Call submissions page, the proposals' ePrint and arXiv
entries and the papers they build on. We then searched ePrint, TCHES, EUROCRYPT and ASIACRYPT with queries such as
``Dilithium rejected signatures ILP'', ``ILWE'' and ``threshold lattice signatures''. We kept a source if it defines or
attacks a threshold ML-DSA design, or supplies a technique against one. We checked the details of every reference online,
and checked Shamir~\cite{shamir} and Feldman~\cite{feldman} against Crossref. For Phase~2 we repeated the search in
October 2026 over ePrint 2024 to 2026 and the preview writeups of the NIST call. This added nine
sources~\cite{ringtail,aommlwe,tanuki,topcoat,mldsaas,meng,descent,noisyleak,haetae} (Section~\ref{sec:recent}).

\textbf{What we read and ran.} We read Quorus~\cite{quorus}, Trilithium~\cite{trilithium} and Mithril~\cite{mithril} in
full. For Mithril this includes Appendices D, E and G at the level of the proof steps. For Kao~\cite{kao} we read the main
text and Appendices B to O. We ran our own harness code (Section~\ref{sec:solution}). Of the schemes' own code we ran only
Mithril's parameter script and its Go signing code. Every TALUS number depends on our model of the channel, because the
v0.22 writeup does not list the values released with each signature. We label every result as measured, derived or
hypothesis.

\section{Literature Survey}

\subsection{Background}
Dilithium~\cite{dilithium} introduced the signing method above, and FIPS~204~\cite{fips204} standardised it. Early lattice
threshold signatures used threshold homomorphic encryption (G\"ur, Katz and Silde~\cite{gks}). Threshold
Raccoon~\cite{raccoon} uses only symmetric tools and one time additive masks, but its signatures are not ML-DSA
signatures. Del Pino and Niot~\cite{finally} give a compact scheme based on Raccoon, limited to $2^{64}$ signatures and
about 8 parties. An earlier preprint on identifiable aborts~\cite{dpia} has been withdrawn and is listed for history only.
The big shift in recent work is toward signatures that pass the unchanged FIPS~204 verifier, because deployed
certificates and pinned keys cannot change.

\textbf{Rounding and the public value $v$.} The hint lets the verifier recompute $w_1$ from $v=Az-ct_12^d$. Putting in
$z=y+cs_1$ gives $v=w-c(s_2-t_0)$. So $v$ is public and contains the term $cs'$, with $s'=s_2-t_0$. The second rejection
check keeps $\LB(w)$ at least $\beta$ away from a rounding boundary, and so $v$ reveals nothing. If the check is removed or
changed, this protection is lost.

\subsection{The four First Call ML-DSA proposals}

\textbf{Mithril}~\cite{mithril,mithrilposter,mithrilpw} comes from PQShield, Brave and Bristol and appears at USENIX
Security 2026. It uses replicated secret sharing with short shares, and each party does its own rejection using hyperball
sampling (sampling from a Euclidean ball). It costs about 1\,MB per participant for up to six parties and has a Go
implementation. Its security proof reduces to MLWE and to the unforgeability of ML-DSA. It allows up to $T-1$ corrupted
parties chosen in advance, without identifiable aborts. Each attempt succeeds with probability about $1/2$ (ML-DSA alone
has about $1/4$), with $K$ parallel repetitions. Each party publishes a full MLWE sample as its commitment, so the bare $Ax$
attack does not apply. The proof uses R\'enyi divergence and covers $Q_s=2^{50}$ signing queries. We do not rely on the
older version~\cite{reloaded}. The preview writeup~\cite{mithrilpw} adds three points. Up to 8 parties remain practical,
no synchronised broadcast channel is needed, and the Go code uses floating point for hyperball sampling.

\textbf{Why rejection by each party is delicate.} A single ML-DSA signer accepts a nonce only if both checks pass, about one
time in four. With $N$ parties that each hold a short additive share, the sum of their answers must land in the same box,
but no party knows the sum. Mithril lets each party draw its nonce share from a hyperball, so that the sum has a
predictable size, and each party decides acceptance on its own. The accepted answers are then close to the FIPS~204
distribution, but not exactly equal to it. How close they are is a question of numbers (problem P2), not a bug.

\textbf{Quorus}~\cite{quorus} comes from J.P.\ Morgan and also appears at USENIX Security 2026. It changes signing so that
it suits MPC (secure multi party computation), while keeping FIPS~204 verification. The nonce is uniform, a small noise
term $e_w$ is added to $w$, and $w_1$ is output even when $(z,h)$ is rejected. It tolerates fewer than $n/2$ corrupted
parties and is proven secure in the universal composability (UC) framework. It costs about 150\,KB per party per attempt,
which is 0.31 to 0.59\,MB per signature over 16 or 29 rounds, and was tested with up to 63 parties. Because $w_1$ is always
released, the Fiat--Shamir hash can be computed in the open. The proof that rejected partial signatures can be simulated
relies on the noise $e_w$.

\textbf{SplitForge/Trilithium}~\cite{trilithium} comes from Cybernetica. It targets a server and a phone, helped by a
correlated randomness provider (CRP). It is UC secure against one malicious party out of the three and takes 14 rounds per
signing attempt. It publishes the high bits $w_H$ even for rejected attempts. This is justified by an MLWR type assumption
(Module Learning With Rounding, which is like MLWE but replaces random noise with fixed rounding). The paper itself calls
this assumption non standard for ML-DSA parameters. Trilithium uses an actively secure comparison protocol and a new
rounding protocol, and has a Rust implementation. It is given both for unchanged ML-DSA and for a Quorus style variant in
which the CRP supplies the noise $e_w$. It is part of Cybernetica's SplitKey submission. The writeup admits that a
malicious CRP can mount selective disclosure attacks, which the authors consider harmless for the intended use.

\textbf{TALUS}~\cite{talus,taluspw} comes from Codebat. It introduces the Boundary Clearance Condition (BCC). For a fixed
share of nonces (43.2\%, 31.7\% and 39.1\% for ML-DSA-44, 65 and 87), $s_2$ provably cannot push $w$ across a rounding
boundary. So rejection can be done offline on prepared nonces, which gives a distributed MPC version with two rounds. The
paper says its cap based protection of the $s_2$ channel is supported by a model rather than proven. It also proves that any
FIPS exact scheme that reveals a summed (Irwin--Hall) nonce allows key recovery after about $2^{30}$ signatures. A one
round variant helped by a TEE (trusted execution environment, a hardware enclave trusted to keep secrets) appears in v0.22
only as a contrasting example. TALUS builds on Kao's Shamir nonce DKG~\cite{kao}. There the signing nonce is itself Shamir
shared, with pairwise cancelling PRF masks, and the paper claims the nonce shares keep more than five times the key's
entropy for signing sets of up to 17.

\subsection{Published attacks on TALUS}\label{sec:attacks}
Niot~\cite{niot} (ePrint 2026/1386) gives two practical attacks on earlier TALUS versions. \textbf{Attack~1} uses
commitments that do not hide. TALUS-MPC broadcast $As_{1,i}$ during key generation and $A\hat y$ in the blame phase. $A$ can
be inverted from the left except with probability at most $2^{-15}$ for ML-DSA-44 or $2^{-38}$ for ML-DSA-65 and 87. So
$x=A^{+}(Ax)$ reveals every key share and the nonce, and $s_1=c^{-1}(z-y)$ follows from one signature. \textbf{Attack~2}
targets the removed $s_2$ check. In simple terms, every signature reveals a very noisy linear equation in the secret, and
enough of them average the noise away. More precisely, $\HB(w)$ is known from the hint, so the attacker sees $b=-cs'+e$,
where $e=\LB(w)$ is roughly uniform on $[-\gamma_2,\gamma_2]$. This is integer LWE (ILWE), which is LWE without reduction
modulo $q$, and least squares solves it~\cite{ilwe}. The error per coefficient is about $\sigma_e/\sqrt{N\tau}$, with
$\sigma_e^2\approx\gamma_2^2/3$. Rounding works once this error is below $1/2$, so the attack needs about
\begin{equation}
N \approx \frac{4\gamma_2^2}{3\tau}\label{eq:ilwe}
\end{equation}
signatures, which is $2^{28}$ to $2^{31}$ (Table~\ref{tab:ilwe}). Niot stresses that (\ref{eq:ilwe}) is not optimised. He
also notes that putting the check back is not enough, because a corrupted party can bias its nonce share (for example
$y_i=0$) so that the check always passes.

The authors' response, v0.22~\cite{taluspw}, replaces the commitments with zero knowledge proofs that the values are well
formed. It handles the $s_2$ channel with a mandatory limit of about $2^{13}$, $2^{14}$ and $2^{14}$ signatures per key
(ML-DSA-44, 65 and 87), enforced by changing the key. The authors compare this cap with an integer uniqueness wall near
$2^{15.2}$, $2^{16.4}$ and $2^{16.2}$. Below the wall, they claim, the observations alone cannot pin down $s_2$. Each
prepared nonce is tied to one quorum and erased after use. The cap is the only defence of the $s_2$ channel, so it is the
natural core target. It also hurts practical use, because certificates and pinned keys cannot be replaced every few
thousand signatures.

\textbf{Attack~1 in detail.} The number theoretic transform (NTT) is the modular version of the FFT. It turns a product of
polynomials in $R_q$ into 256 separate products of numbers modulo $q$, one per slot. In the NTT domain, $A$ splits into 256
separate $k\times\ell$ matrices over $\mathbb{Z}_q$, one per slot. Each one can be inverted from the left unless an event of
probability about $1/q$ happens, and this is where the $2^{-15}$ and $2^{-38}$ bounds come from. So any observer solves the
system slot by slot and gets $s_{1,i}$ exactly. Shortness plays no role, because no lattice problem is involved. With
$k=\ell=1$ the commitment is just $a\cdot s\bmod q$, and dividing by $a$ gives back $s$. The tempting reasoning is that
discrete log commitments hide, so lattice ones should too. It fails because $g^x$ hides $x$ only because discrete logarithms
are hard, while $Ax$ is a linear map, and linear maps are easy to undo.

\textbf{Attack~2 in detail.} Look at one coefficient of $s'$. A single sample $b=-cs'+e$ places a sum of $\tau$ secret
coefficients in a window about $2\gamma_2$ wide. This is far wider than the range of the secret, so one sample says almost
nothing. Averaging over many independent challenges shrinks the uncertainty like $1/\sqrt{N\tau}$. Once it falls below
$1/2$, each coefficient can be rounded. The factor $\gamma_2^2/\tau$ in (\ref{eq:ilwe}) shows why ML-DSA-44, with its small
$\gamma_2=(q-1)/88$, leaks the most per signature. Two points matter for P1. Formula (\ref{eq:ilwe}) treats the noise as
Gaussian and so wastes the information in its sharp edges. It also recovers $s'$ one coefficient at a time, while integer
programming and lattice reduction use all the constraints together.

\subsection{Leakage attacks on lattice signatures}\label{sec:toolbox}
Bootle et al.~\cite{ilwe} introduced the ILWE view of linear leakage in BLISS. Zhou et al.~\cite{rejected} show that the
challenges of rejected Dilithium signatures bound $cs$ from both sides, and they recover keys by integer linear programming
(ILP). Damm et al.~\cite{concealed} define Concealed ILWE, where only some samples leak, and break masked Dilithium with
Huber and Cauchy regression. Yates et al.~\cite{yates} find that least squares attacks on captured signatures are not
enough at the parameters they studied. So any threshold design that releases values linked to $cs_1$ or $cs_2$ must be
tested with estimators stronger than least squares. These include bounded noise estimators, ILP, robust regression and
lattice reduction.

\textbf{Why a summed nonce leaks.} Suppose each of $T$ parties adds a uniform nonce share. The total then follows a bell
shaped Irwin--Hall distribution, and $z=y+cs_1$ is that bell moved by $cs_1$. So each signature carries a little Fisher
information about $s_1$. Kao~\cite{kao} bounds the loss below 0.013 bits per signature, but~\cite{talus} proves an attack
after about $2^{30}$ signatures at ML-DSA-65. A design must therefore either make the revealed nonce exactly uniform, as
Quorus and Trilithium claim, or limit the number of signatures, as TALUS does.

\textbf{Corruption models.} With static corruption the attacker picks the corrupted parties before the protocol starts.
With adaptive corruption it can pick them while the protocol runs. Dishonest majority security, as in Mithril, allows
$T-1$ corrupted signers. Honest majority security, as in Quorus and TALUS-MPC, needs $N\ge2T-1$. Without identifiable
aborts, as in Mithril, a corrupted party can abort after seeing the honest parties' messages and try again. The aborted
transcripts then become part of what the attacker sees. For this reason the harness treats the corruption model as an
input, not a fixed setting.

\textbf{Countermeasures.} Masking splits secrets into shares so that no value in between is linked to the key.
But~\cite{concealed} shows that masking alone fails when some samples leak with little noise. Against leakage from
rejection, the defence is to hide which check failed and to release nothing computed from a rejected answer. This is why
Quorus needs a careful simulation argument for releasing $(w_1,c,\bot)$. Each defence has a cost. Masking costs time, a
signing cap costs key changes, and hiding rejected transcripts costs extra interaction.

\subsection{Related and older work}
The Mithril line began as a poster~\cite{mithrilposter} and an early ePrint that has since been
withdrawn~\cite{reloaded}. All Mithril claims here come from~\cite{mithril,mithrilpw}. Kao's design~\cite{kao} is not a
Call submission, but TALUS builds on it and it shows the same patterns. Its fully distributed profile P2 broadcasts masked
commitments. The paper's own remarks show that an exposed value $\lambda_hAy_h$ gives $y_h$ and then the key. The masks
hide these values only when $|S\setminus C|\ge2$, that is, when at least two signers are honest. The paper admits that mask
hiding fails in P2 at $T=N$ with $N-1$ corrupted parties. Yet its Table~1 still claims unforgeability with a dishonest
majority, with UC proofs against static attackers. Its Irwin--Hall analysis bounds the loss by 0.013 bits per signing
query and calls this meaningful for $q_s<16{,}000$.

\subsection{Recent work from 2024 to 2026 (added in Phase~2)}\label{sec:recent}
\textbf{Lattice threshold designs that give up ML-DSA compatibility.} Espitau, Katsumata and Takemure~\cite{aommlwe} give a
two round lattice threshold signature under a new assumption, algebraic one more MLWE. Its signatures are about 11\,KB even
for 1024 signers. Ringtail~\cite{ringtail} reaches two rounds from standard LWE. Its first round does not depend on the
message, its signatures are 13.4\,KB, it needs 10.5\,KB of online communication at 1024 parties, and it was run across 8
countries. These authors have joined forces in Tanuki~\cite{tanuki}, a two round scheme based on Raccoon that was announced
for the same NIST call. Its signatures are not ML-DSA signatures, so it falls outside our four targets. Together these
schemes show the price of FIPS~204 compatibility. Giving it up buys fewer rounds and thousands of parties.
TOPCOAT~\cite{topcoat}, from the Cybernetica group behind Trilithium, is an earlier two party Dilithium protocol with
public key compression and fewer rounds, based on MLWE and MSIS.

\textbf{Follow ups to the ML-DSA proposals.} Rambaud, Roth and Urban~\cite{mldsaas} turn Mithril into a ``threshold
signatures as a service'' variant called ML-DSaaS. It merges the first two rounds into one round that does not depend on
the message, and reports an online phase two to three times faster. A preprint by Meng~\cite{meng}, posted on 20 October
2026 after our experiments, studies nonce repetition caused by faults. If a party's device is forced to reuse its nonce
across sessions, the responses it reveals expose its share. The preprint reports exact key recovery on Mithril. It rates
Quorus as immune, because responses of single parties are never revealed. It rates TALUS as exposed only if the one time
preparation is skipped, and Trilithium as fragile under faults. This is a physical fault model, outside our model in which
the attacker sees only transcripts. We read only its abstract page and have not reproduced it.

\textbf{Better leakage estimators for ML-DSA.} Two 2026 preprints improve the toolbox of Section~\ref{sec:toolbox} for a
single ML-DSA signer when one bit of the nonce leaks with each signature. Schubert et al.~\cite{descent} replace linear
regression with constraint solving and hill climbing. They need 37 to 68 times fewer relations, between 5{,}000 and
35{,}000. Paskarbeit et al.~\cite{noisyleak} estimate the bit error rate of noisy leakage and cut the relations needed by
20 to 44\%. Both agree with the lesson of our P1 result. Estimators that use the exact structure of the leakage beat plain
least squares by orders of magnitude. Finally, HAETAE~\cite{haetae} is the main other Fiat--Shamir with aborts signature
built on hyperball sampling. Section~\ref{sec:mithril} uses it to check Mithril's intersection bound.

\subsection{Gaps and comparison}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No systematic public testing} of Mithril, Quorus or Trilithium transcripts. There are only arguments at the
design level.
\item \textbf{The TALUS cap} sits about 2 bits below the authors' wall and 14 to 17 bits below Niot's unoptimised
estimate (\ref{eq:ilwe}). A better bounded noise or ILP estimator could close this gap.
\item \textbf{Rejected attempt transcripts} (Quorus, Trilithium) and \textbf{Mithril's accepted distribution and hint loss}
(7 to 12 bits reported, proof for $Q_s=2^{50}$ only) have not been checked independently.
\item \textbf{Gaps in the corruption model.} Some proofs cover static corruption only, some need an honest majority, and
some have side conditions such as $|S\setminus C|\ge2$.
\end{itemize}
Only Mithril tolerates a dishonest majority, but it is limited to $N\le6$. Quorus scales, but needs an honest majority and
many rounds. Trilithium covers a phone and a server with a trusted helper. TALUS uses the fewest rounds, but its $s_2$
protection is a usage limit rather than a structural argument. Independent testing therefore helps most where a proof
contains a margin (TALUS), a heuristic (Trilithium) or a changed distribution (Mithril). The quality of the evidence also
differs. Mithril and Quorus have reductions in the (quantum) random oracle model. Trilithium has a UC proof that rests on an
MLWR type assumption. TALUS has an information theoretic argument with a numerical margin. A margin is the one kind of
evidence that an experiment can check directly. This is one more reason to make the TALUS cap the core problem.

\textbf{Mithril.} (i) Loss through accepted answers beyond $2^{50}$ queries. Confirmed as a proof gap. Under the paper's own
theorem the published parameters give $Q_s=2^{26}$ to $2^{37}$ (Section~\ref{sec:mithril}). (ii) Hint loss of 7 to 12

selective aborts. Not examined further. (v) Floating point sampler. We ran the Go code, and its transcripts show no $s_2$

\textbf{Quorus.} (i) Revealing values on rejection is proven to be simulatable. Confirmed, but the proof is computational
(based on MLWE), not statistical. We also correct our own earlier wording. $Ay+e_w$ is uniform for each coefficient on its
own, not as a whole vector, so only simple statistical tests are ruled out. (ii) Only $\bot$ is revealed. Confirmed. The
batched OR step has 40 bit soundness. (iii) Building blocks. We found two text errors (Section~\ref{sec:p3}), not attacks.
(iv) Honest majority boundary. Narrowed, since the paper treats only $n=2t+1$.

\textbf{Trilithium.} (i) Revised. After a rejection the released value is the commitment $w_H$, not a partial signature,
under an explicit MLWR type assumption. (ii) One declassified bit per attempt, as in ML-DSA retries. Confirmed. (iii) A
malicious server that tampers with the rejection check. Confirmed as a simulation gap that is visible in the paper's own
proof. It is worth at most about $\log_2(\text{attempts})$ bits, once. (iv) The CRP is trusted, and a CRP that colludes
with the server is fatal by design.

\textbf{TALUS v0.22.} The cap is the whole $s_2$ argument and the noise has sharp edges. We measure this in
Section~\ref{sec:p1}. \textbf{Kao P2.} The hypothesis is strengthened to a key exposure, shown at full ML-DSA size
(Section~\ref{sec:kao}).

\section{Problem Formulation}

\subsection{Threat model and notation}
A $(T,N)$ threshold scheme $\Pi$ has a transcript distribution $\mathrm{Tr}_\Pi(\mathit{sk},m)$. This is everything an
attacker sees while $m$ is signed, including broadcasts, the state of corrupted parties and aborted attempts. The attacker
corrupts, in advance, as many parties as the scheme allows. It watches $q_s$ signing sessions, and it wins if it recovers
the secret. Recovering $s_1$ is enough, since $s_2$ then follows from $t$. We study key recovery because it is stronger
evidence than a small statistical bias. Table~\ref{tab:notation} adds the notation not defined in Section~I. What each

\subsection{Core problem P1, the TALUS signing cap}
\textbf{The idea.} TALUS removes the $s_2$ check, so each signature leaks one noisy equation in $s'$. The defence is to stop
before enough equations pile up. BCC keeps the noise $e$ inside $(-\gamma_2+\beta,\gamma_2-\beta)$, so the noise has hard
edges. A single sample near an edge rules out many candidate secrets. So a bounded noise estimator may need far fewer
samples than least squares, whose error falls only like $1/\sqrt N$. \textbf{Statement.} We are given observations
$b_j=-c_js'+e_j$, with each $e_j$ inside the BCC interval. For each parameter set, find the number of signatures $N^*$ at
which $s'$ is recovered, and checked against the public key, with probability 0.95. Then compare $\log_2N^*$ with the cap
and the wall. A value below the cap is a break. A value above it gives a margin of $\log_2N^*-\log_2\text{cap}$ bits.
\textbf{Success criterion.} $N^*$ with stated trials and seeds, a positive control (the estimator recovers a leaky
variant) and a negative control (no false keys on genuine ML-DSA).

\textbf{Why the cap needs measuring.} The public key already determines $s_2$, so below the wall the protection is
computational. The authors model a meet in the middle attack on the syndrome, with cost $2^{H/2}$ and leftover entropy
$H\ge502$ (about $2^{160}$). But the only proven floor is about $2^{58}$ at ML-DSA-65 and $2^{49}$ at ML-DSA-87, which is
below 128 bits. ML-DSA-44 has no public cokernel at all, since $k=\ell$. Above the wall the observations alone determine
$s'$. So an efficient estimator that works there recovers the key without any lattice work. Between the cap and the wall,
safety rests on that unproven model. This is why P1 measures $N^*$ directly. \textbf{Hypothesis (stated in Phase~1).}
Bounded noise and ILP estimators beat the least squares estimate (\ref{eq:ilwe}) by a wide margin.

\subsection{Stretch goals P2 to P4}
\textbf{P2 (Mithril).} Check the $Q_s=2^{50}$ accounting, and the hint loss from key sharing for the exact set of shares
held by $T-1$ corrupted parties. \textbf{P3 (rejected attempts).} Test whether the transcripts of rejected attempts in
Quorus and Trilithium depend on $s_1$ or $s_2$. \textbf{P4 (Kao P2).} Decide whether the masked commitments hide $y$ when
$|S\setminus C|=1$, as the paper's Table~1 implies. Our Phase~1 hypotheses were as follows. For P2, a wall above $2^{64}$
(no attack) with the hint loss worked out again. For P3, Quorus can be simulated thanks to $e_w$, while Trilithium's
assumption holds only roughly. For P4, the masks fail at $|S\setminus C|=1$. The success criteria were a numerical bound
with its derivation (P2), a statistical test with a stated false positive rate and sample budget (P3), and an explicit key
recovery on a toy instance or a reason why the masks hide (P4).

\section{Proposed Solution, a Leakage Testing Harness}\label{sec:solution}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.68\columnwidth]{harness_architecture.png}
\caption{Harness architecture. Our ML-DSA core produces every signing attempt with its secret internals. A scheme view
keeps only what that scheme releases, so secrets stop there. An adapter turns the observations into pairs $(c,b)$. The
estimators never see the secret, and the experiments score them against the true key.}\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Design (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}).} The harness has three parts. (1)~\textbf{ML-DSA core.} It covers the NTT, SHAKE
sampling, hints, and signing and verification, and it keeps every attempt. It matches the dilithium-py implementation byte
for byte and passes 81 official NIST ACVP vectors for key generation, signing and verification, for all three parameter
sets. (2)~\textbf{Scheme views.} Plain ML-DSA (negative control), a variant that leaks $y$ (positive control), TALUS-BCC,
TALUS v0.1 and Mithril, plus separate simulators for Quorus, Trilithium and Kao. (3)~\textbf{Estimators.} Least squares. A
bounded noise ``edge'' estimator, written as a Chebyshev linear program that makes the largest residual $|b+cs'|$ as small
as possible, and solved on CPU or GPU. An ILP finishing step that searches for an integer $s'$ that fits every bound. And
theory curves from Fisher information and Cram\'er--Rao bounds. Every recovery is checked against the true key, and every
run is seeded and logged.

\textbf{How the estimators work.} Least squares averages. Stacking $N$ samples $(c,b)$ gives an integer system with more
equations than unknowns. Its solution has an error variance of about $\sigma_e^2/(N\tau)$ per coefficient, so the error
falls like $1/\sqrt N$. The bounded noise estimator uses the edges instead. If every $|e|$ is at most a known bound, each
sample gives a pair of linear inequalities, $|b+cs'|\le$ bound. The region that satisfies all of them is a polytope, and it
shrinks roughly like $1/N$ rather than $1/\sqrt N$. The Chebyshev linear program finds its centre, and rounding gives $s'$.
ILP finishing replaces that rounding with an integer search inside the polytope, in the style of~\cite{rejected}. This helps
when a few coefficients sit near a rounding boundary. The Fisher information $I$ per signature sets the Cram\'er--Rao bound
on the variance of any unbiased estimator. Key recovery then needs on the order of $4/(I\tau)$ signatures~\cite{talus}. We
keep robust regression (Huber, Cauchy~\cite{concealed}) and hint accounting with the lattice estimator for schemes where
only some samples leak, or where corrupted parties hold partial information.

\textbf{A view in action (TALUS).} For each signing attempt the core records $y$, $w$, $c$, $z$ and which check failed. The
TALUS view does three things. (1) It keeps only attempts whose nonce satisfies BCC, as the offline filter would. (2) It
drops the secret fields. (3) It outputs what an observer can compute, $v=Az-ct_12^d$ and the hint, together with $c$. The
adapter then forms $b=-cs'+e$. A version with a corrupted group of parties would add their shares. For schemes with aborts,
the view outputs exactly the fields that the specification says a rejected attempt reveals, and nothing more. The estimator
receives only the view's output, so any key it recovers could be recovered from public data.

\textbf{Implementation.} The code is Python with numpy and scipy. The linear programs use the HiGHS solver. The ring
products inside the edge estimator take about 90\% of its run time. They run on an NVIDIA GPU with PyTorch in double
precision, about three times faster than the CPU version, and we checked them against it. Least squares is solved exactly
in one pass. After an FFT the equations for each frequency are independent, so each frequency needs a single division. This
agrees with an iterative solver to $10^{-13}$. Transcripts for several trials are built in parallel processes while the GPU
solves.

\textbf{Evaluation and validity.} Every experiment reports the signatures used, the fraction recovered, the cost and the
parameter set. $N^*$ is the point where a logistic fit to the success counts reaches 0.95. We use 20 trials per $N$ and
Clopper--Pearson intervals. Recovery is checked against the true key, so a partial match is never counted as a break. Two
kinds of risk remain. Internal risks, such as bugs in the primitives or an estimator that overfits, are handled by the ACVP
vectors and by many random keys. External risks, such as our simulator differing from the real protocol or parameters
changing before the packages appear, remain. This is why every TALUS number is labelled as based on our model. A negative
result is stated only as a bound on what we tried.

\textbf{End to end check (gate B).} We passed real ML-DSA-44 attempts through the TALUS view and the unchanged estimator.
It recovered $s'$ for 2 of 2 keys at $N=2\times10^5$ and $4\times10^5$ accepted signatures, and for 0 of 2 at $10^5$ (seed
2029). This agrees with $N^*$ in Table~\ref{tab:margin}.

\textbf{Least squares calibration (Fig.~\ref{fig:ls}).} To check the simulator and the error law behind (\ref{eq:ilwe}),
we measured the least squares error on ML-DSA-44 with $n=256$ (one ring element) and plain $\LB$ noise. We used
$N\in\{10^3,3\cdot10^3,10^4,3\cdot10^4,10^5,3\cdot10^5\}$, 10 trials per $N$ and seed 2033 (generator
\texttt{default\_rng([2033,N,trial])}). The mean RMS error stays within 2\% of $\gamma_2/\sqrt{3\tau N}$ at every $N$. Real
TALUS v0.1 signatures (keys from seeds 101 to 103) fall on the same curve. Extending the curve, rounding every coefficient
correctly needs on the order of $10^9$ signatures. So least squares alone is far from the cap, and the bounded noise
estimator is what matters for P1.

\textbf{Why our method is better than the baseline (Fig.~\ref{fig:success}).} Least squares is the estimator behind Niot's
attack (\ref{eq:ilwe}). On the same transcripts it recovers nothing up to $2^{17.8}$ signatures, and in our model it needs
about $2^{31.7}$. The edge estimator reaches 100\% at $2^{17.2}$. ILP finishing moves the curve about 1.1 bits further left,
to 95\% at $2^{16.0}$ for one element. That is a gain of about $2^{15.7}$ over the baseline, or roughly $5\times10^4$ times
fewer signatures. The gain comes from using the known hard edges of the noise instead of averaging them away. The whole
secret curve sits only about 0.1 bit to the right of the one element curve.

\textbf{Statistics.} Each point uses 20 independent trials (seed 2041, ILP time limit 120\,s), with a logistic fit to the
success counts. For one ring element of ML-DSA-44 we see 0 of 20 at $2^{15.61}$, 11 of 20 at $2^{15.87}$, 19 of 20 at
$2^{15.99}$, and 20 of 20 from $2^{16.10}$. ML-DSA-65 gives 16 of 20 at $2^{17.29}$ and 20 of 20 at $2^{17.71}$. ML-DSA-87
gives 18 of 20 at $2^{17.18}$ and 20 of 20 at $2^{17.49}$. \textbf{Whole secret.} An attacker needs all $k$ elements from
the same signatures. If the elements are independent, 95\% success for the whole secret needs $p_\mathrm{elem}^k=0.95$,
where $p_\mathrm{elem}$ is the success rate for one element. For ML-DSA-44 we ran it (seed 2081, 20 whole secrets per $N$).
We got 3 of 20 at $2^{15.92}$, 15 of 20 at $2^{16.01}$ and 18 of 20 at $2^{16.10}$. These lie inside the intervals of the
predicted 0.26, 0.77 and 0.90, so the extrapolations for ML-DSA-65 and 87 can be trusted. Without ILP, with plain rounding,
the points sit about 1 bit higher (for ML-DSA-44, $N^*_{99}=1.45\times10^5$). \textbf{Controls.} We ran real ML-DSA-44
signing without the $r_0$ check, which is the second rejection check of Section~I on $r_0=\LB(w-cs_2)$. The estimator
recovered the secret element $s_2-t_0$ (one ring element) in 9 of 9 runs (3 keys, each at $N=2$, 4 and $8\times10^5$). On
genuine ML-DSA it returned the secret for 0 of 18 keys (15 of them from seed 2027). It only ever returned $-t_0$, which is
already equivalent to public data. So the false positive rate is below about 15\% at 95\% confidence
(Table~\ref{tab:controls}). \textbf{Verdict.} No break. The margin above the cap is about 3 to 3.5 bits, and above the
authors' wall only about 1 bit. This is much thinner than the 12 to 15 bits that (\ref{eq:ilwe}) suggests.

\textbf{Other ways to attack the cap, all closed.} (a) Changing the key is a fresh key generation (v0.22 Sec.~2.3), not a

\textbf{From toy rings to full size.} We first ran the edge estimator on smaller rings, $n=8$ to 128, with 10 to 20 trials
per point and ML-DSA-44 numbers (Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}). $N^*$ per element levelled off around 1 to $2\times10^5$
($2.8\times10^5$ at $n=64$ and $1.9\times10^5$ at $n=128$) instead of falling toward the cap. This is why we then made the
full size runs above denser. At $n=64$ the harder target $s_2-t_0$ needed the same $N^*$ as $s_2$, and plain $\LB$ noise
behaved like BCC noise. Compared with plain least squares (about $3.6\times10^9$ in our model), the edge estimator gains
about four orders of magnitude. Without ILP, however, it stays 4 to 4.5 bits above the cap. The least squares figure is
about 12 times estimate (\ref{eq:ilwe}), which is $3.1\times10^8$, for a simple reason. Formula (\ref{eq:ilwe}) only asks
that the typical error per coefficient fall to $1/2$. Rounding all 256 coefficients correctly at once needs the error to be
about 3.5 standard deviations inside $1/2$. Since the error shrinks like $1/\sqrt N$, this costs a factor of about
$3.5^2\approx12$ in $N$. The theory module explains the shape. For one coefficient alone, the error under uniform noise
falls exactly like $1/N$, which we checked by Monte Carlo. When all 256 coefficients are solved together, a fitted coupling
constant of about $0.76\,\tau^{0.42}$ explains why the real $N^*$ sits 1.5 to 2 bits above the single coefficient formula.
ILP finishing lowers the 50\% point by a factor of 1.0, 1.1, 1.1 and 1.5 at $n=8$, 16, 32 and 64. This fits the factor of
about 2 at $n=256$.

\textbf{Limits of the P1 result.} (i) The channel model is our reading of TALUS, not a quote from the specification. It
assumes independent uniform noise on the BCC interval, no wrap around, and no use of the hint. (ii) We ran the whole secret
experiment for ML-DSA-44 only. ML-DSA-65 and 87 are extrapolated. (iii) We did not use the hint $h$ separately, and the
lattice estimate is a Python core-SVP model. Core-SVP counts only the cost of the single shortest vector search inside
lattice reduction, which is the usual cautious way to price lattice attacks. So $N^*$ is an upper bound on what an attacker
needs. (iv) The wall and cap figures are the authors'. We did not work out the wall again ourselves.

\subsection{TALUS v0.1 and Mithril}\label{sec:mithril}
\textbf{TALUS v0.1 (Niot's attacks reproduced).} We implemented the arXiv v2 design~\cite{talus} with ML-DSA-44, $|S|=3$,
keys from seeds 101 to 103, and a DKG simplified to Shamir sharing. Attack~1 recovered $s_1$ exactly from the key
generation broadcast (3 of 3 keys) and from one MPC signature (60 of 60). Attack~2 by least squares follows the theory curve
up to $N=10^5$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ls}) and would need about $1.2\times10^9$ signatures. A separate check on real ML-DSA-44
transcripts without the check gave 0.97 times Niot's error formula (12 runs). On the same transcripts the edge estimator
needed about 1{,}500 times fewer signatures than his least squares $N$. Our simplifications were plain ML-DSA key
generation followed by Shamir sharing of $s_1$ instead of the paper's DKG, the MPC computation of $w_1$ replaced by its
correct output, and $|S|=T=3$ throughout. The exercise shows why TALUS changed its design. It also shows that our views
reproduce a published break exactly.

\textbf{Mithril proof parameter gap (P2).} Theorem~3.2 of~\cite{mithril} bounds the number of safe queries using a lemma on
the intersection of hyperballs. The lemma is taken from the ePrint full version of Devevey et al.~\cite{devevey}
(App.~A.6). That bound uses the exponent $n-1$, where a correct cylinder argument gives $(n-1)/2$. For ML-DSA-44 it gives
$2^{-50.1}$, while the exact value is $2^{-33.5}$. We reproduced this with the authors' own Sage parameter script. With the
published parameters the theorem then covers $Q_s=2^{26}$ to $2^{37}$ queries rather than $2^{50}$. Under the typical norm
distribution, which is a heuristic and not a proof, most sets still reach $2^{50}$, and some fall up to about 3 bits short.
Corrected parameters need 1.3 to 8.5 times more parallel repetitions $K$ (Table~\ref{tab:mithrilqs}). The faulty bound
appears only in the ePrint version, since the ASIACRYPT version states only the exact formula. Of 13 citing works we
checked, only Mithril uses it. HAETAE~\cite{haetae}, the other main hyperball scheme, computes the exact incomplete Beta
value numerically and is not affected. Seen the other way, reaching $2^{50}$ with the published radii would need the norm
bound $B$ in the proof to sit only 0 to 7 standard deviations above the mean norm of $cs$. The proof, however, needs an
overwhelming bound, and the paper's footnote says about 13. This is a proof gap, not an attack. Going slightly over the
bound enlarges a R\'enyi divergence budget a little and does not obviously reveal $s$. We wrote a corrected bound with a
proof. It uses the exponent $(n-1)/2$ and is within 0.04 bits of the exact value at Mithril sizes. It held at all 320 test
points, while the printed bound failed at 149. \textbf{Real transcripts.} We ran the authors' Go signing code (ML-DSA-44,
$(T,N)=(2,3)$, 110{,}000 slots, of which 39{,}461 had no party rejecting). Regressing the leftover of $Az-ct-w$ on $-cs_2$
gives a slope of $0.004\pm0.10$, where 1 would mean an $s_2$ leak. So accepted answers do not depend on $s_2$. Only $ct_0$
remains, which is equivalent to public data, and we corrected our Mithril view to match. \textbf{Hint loss.} For the exact
set of shares held by $T-1$ parties, the lattice estimator gives losses of 4.9 to 7.7 bits (ML-DSA-44), 17.7 to 23.9 bits
(ML-DSA-65) and 8.6 to 14.4 bits (ML-DSA-87), compared with the reported 7 to 12 bits (Table~\ref{tab:hint}).

\textbf{Beyond $2^{50}$ queries.} Signature schemes are usually assumed to face up to $2^{64}$ queries. In Mithril the
accepted answer is uniform on an intersection of balls. So the R\'enyi divergence has the closed form $1/(1-\delta)$ at any
order, and the shape alone causes no Fisher information leak. But at $2^{64}$ queries every published set falls short of
what the proof needs, by 27 to 38 bits under the theorem and by 3 to 17 bits under the typical case heuristic. Parameters
corrected for $2^{64}$ need $K$ to be 1.7 to 23 times larger. Correcting only the faulty lemma at $2^{50}$ lowers the radius
ratio $\varphi$ from 7 to 9 down to 5.3 to 7.7, and raises $K$ by 1.3 to 8.5 times. The worst case is ML-DSA-65 with
$(T,N)=(5,6)$, where $K$ goes from 1{,}200 to 10{,}234. Communication grows with $K$. These figures are computed from the
paper's formulas, not simulated.

The Python core-SVP substitute reproduces the paper's 12 bits for ML-DSA-44 when given the paper's constant 0.74. The
figure we quote is from the lattice estimator run (Sage 10.4, rough mode). Only ML-DSA-65 clearly goes beyond the claimed
range. Both Mithril items are accounting and proof issues, and neither gives an attack on the scheme.

\subsection{Kao's P2 profile (P4)}\label{sec:kao}
In the paper's own text (Fig.~1b, Remark~11, App.~M.1), every party broadcasts $W_i$, and the pairwise masks cancel
(Lemma~1). So $\sum_i W_i=Ay$ is public. Gaussian elimination gives $y$, and then $s_1=c^{-1}(z-y)$ from a single P2
signature, whatever $|S\setminus C|$ is. The proof of Corollary~5 itself calls this value the public broadcast, and no later
appendix changes it. \textbf{Shown at full size.} We re-implemented the scheme at the real ML-DSA-44, 65 and 87 sizes. The
combined signatures pass the standard verifier, and we recovered $s_1$ exactly from one signature for 15 of 15 keys per set
(Table~\ref{tab:kao}). We used $(N,T)=(3,2)$, $(5,3)$ and $(5,5)$, five keys each, seed 2051, and each attack took under
0.2\,s. Holding back a single $W_i$ drops this to 0 of 15, so the attack really uses the sum. The author's code is not
public, and our rejection checks run in the open rather than inside the paper's MPC. Our earlier toy version (ring size 8,
real $q$) also checks the narrower Phase~1 hypothesis. With all signers but one corrupted, the honest party's mask can be
removed and $\lambda_hAy_h$ inverted. With two honest signers this route fails, as the paper says. The sum route above,
however, works whatever the number of honest parties. Two more points. Corollary~6 claims the full threshold signature
functionality without the condition $|H|\ge2$ that its Simulator~17 needs. And the key generation commitment
$t^{(i)}=As_1^{(i)}+s_2^{(i)}$ carries the noise $s_2^{(i)}$, so the bare $As_{1,i}$ pattern broken in TALUS does not appear
there.

\subsection{Quorus and Trilithium (P3)}\label{sec:p3}
Our Quorus implementation produced 272{,}664 signatures, and none failed FIPS~204 verification. By construction no
statistical leak is possible at the level of single coefficients. We did not run a distinguisher at the lattice level. For
Trilithium we used exact enumeration at $n=4$ and Monte Carlo at $n=8$ ($2\times10^8$ samples, 3 trials). Both show the gap
between the rejected $w_H$ and the simulator shrinking as $\gamma_1$ and $n$ grow. For branch~2 at $n=8$ it goes from
$3\times10^{-5}$ to $7\times10^{-7}$. We also found errors in the papers, which are not attacks. Quorus Functionality~6
uses the bound $\gamma_2-\beta-\eta$ where every other place uses $\gamma_2-\beta$ (PDF p.~32). Quorus Lemma~8 prints the
denominator $2(\beta+1)\delta+1$ instead of $(2\beta+1)\delta+1$. An exhaustive check shows that the printed probabilities
sum to only 0.84 to 0.95. Trilithium's simulation of unsuccessful signing (p.~24) leaves the gap noted in
Section~\ref{sec:surface}.

\textbf{Trilithium in more detail.} After a rejection, the released values come from two branches. In branch~3 the $z$
check failed. In branch~2 the $z$ check passed but the $\LB$ check failed. Exact enumeration at $n=4$ showed a real but
small gap between the rejected $w_H$ and the simulator. It shrinks as $\gamma_1$ grows, roughly like $\gamma_1^{-2.7}$ for
branch~3. A branch~2 floor near $10^{-4}$ remained for $\gamma_1\ge\gamma_2$ at $q=257$, 513 and 1025, so it is not caused by
a small $q$. It comes from the rounding cell that wraps around, and masking that cell removes it (30 to 65 times smaller).
Exact convolution for $n=2$ to 6 at $q=17$ shows both gaps falling as $n$ grows. Branch~3 falls by 2 to 3 orders of
magnitude from $n=4$ to 6 (Fig.~\ref{fig:trigap}). The figure also shows that the trend is not steady from $n=3$ to 4. At
$n=8$ the branch~3 gap is below the Monte Carlo floor of about $3\times10^{-7}$. Toy sizes cannot prove anything about
$n=256$, but every trend points toward the assumption holding statistically. A distinguisher at the lattice level remains
untested. On the server tampering issue (H-F3), a server that tampers with the rejection check is detected and stopped. So
the leak is bounded by the stopping time, about $\log_2(\text{attempts})$ bits, once. The problem is that the paper's
simulation does not reproduce how that stopping probability depends on the real $z$.

\textbf{Quorus in more detail.} Apart from the two text errors, the batched OR protocol has statistical soundness $2^{-39}$
($\kappa=40$). So about one rejected attempt in $2^{39}$ is wrongly declared a pass, and its failing $z$, which depends on
the key, is opened. At roughly $10^{11}$ signatures this is not practical, but 40 bit statistical security is lower than
usual. A rough reduction, which is ours and was not run, suggests that the MLWE instance behind Theorem~2 is about as hard
as recovering the ML-DSA key itself.

\section{Phase 3 Prototype and Conclusion}
\textbf{What the Phase 3 prototype will show.} It will be a complete tool for measuring the TALUS cap margin. It takes
transcripts produced by the TALUS reference code (packages expected from March 2027) and passes them through a scheme view.
It then runs the bounded noise estimator with ILP finishing and reports $N^*$ and the margin in bits against the cap and the
wall. It also runs the positive and negative controls automatically, so a margin is never reported without them. In
concrete terms it will (i) replace our channel model with the values the real code releases, (ii) run the whole secret ILP
for ML-DSA-65 and 87 instead of extrapolating, and (iii) rerun the Mithril parameter check on the submitted parameters.
Success means reproducing Table~\ref{tab:margin} on real transcripts, or explaining any difference, with each margin
reported together with its controls. Until the packages appear, the prototype runs on our TALUS view, so the demonstration
does not depend on the release date. The demonstration will be a single command. It takes a transcript file and a parameter
set and prints the margin table with its controls, trial counts and seeds. The stretch goals continue alongside it. These
are a lattice level distinguisher for rejected transcripts, and disclosure to the Kao and Mithril authors.

\textbf{Research questions, answered.} (RQ1) Which threshold ML-DSA designs exist? There are four First Call proposals.
They differ in corruption model, number of parties and rounds (Table~\ref{tab:schemes}). (RQ2) Which are broken? Earlier
TALUS versions, by two attacks that we reproduced. Kao's P2 profile also exposes the key from one signature, going by the
paper's own text. (RQ3) Which techniques work? Linear inversion of a published $Ax$, recovery from a leaked nonce, and for
the TALUS channel a bounded noise estimator with ILP finishing. The last is about four orders of magnitude better than least
squares. (RQ4) Where is the evidence thinnest? The TALUS cap margin, now measured at about 3 to 3.5 bits above the cap.
Mithril's accounting between parameters and proof. And the untested question at the lattice level for rejected transcripts
in Quorus and Trilithium.

\textbf{Lessons from Phase~2.} Three points about method carry over to Phase~3. First, controls change conclusions. Without
the negative control, the estimator's output $-t_0$ on genuine ML-DSA could have been mistaken for leakage. Second, runs at
reduced size can mislead in either direction. The ML-DSA-87 shortcut at $n=64$ overstated the margin, and the ILP gains grow
with $n$. So we quote only full size numbers. Third, reading the papers in full corrected two of our own Phase~1 statements.
Trilithium releases $w_H$, not a partial signature, and uniformity holds only for each coefficient on its own. This is why
every hypothesis in Section~\ref{sec:surface} now has a verdict.

\textbf{How this work differs from earlier work.} Earlier cryptanalysis of lattice
signatures~\cite{ilwe,rejected,concealed,yates} targets a single signer that leaks through a physical side channel. Here
the leakage is logical. It is part of the protocol transcript, any observer or corrupted party can see it, and its noise
distribution is known exactly from the specification. So our results carry over across implementations, and schemes that
were analysed only by proof gain an independent check by experiment.

\textbf{Ethics.} We study only public specifications at the standardisation stage, as NIST invites. We share findings
about a scheme with its authors before any public posting. We keep confirmed key recovery, measured margins, proof gaps and
errata apart, and we label results that rely on our simulators rather than on the authors' code.

\textbf{Conclusion.} The survey of 33 sources showed that threshold ML-DSA designs fail when patterns from discrete log
protocols are copied over, and that three of the four proposals had not been tested. In Phase~2 we built and checked a
harness and measured the core quantity. The TALUS cap holds in our model, but only by about 3 to 3.5 bits for the whole
secret and about 1 bit above the authors' wall. This is much less than least squares suggests. Insider, coordinator, key
change and lattice finishing routes do not lower it. Our other findings are a gap between Mithril's parameters and its
proof, a key exposure in Kao's P2 profile, and errata in Quorus and Trilithium. We will disclose these to the authors before
any public posting.
