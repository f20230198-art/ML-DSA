\begin{abstract}
ML-DSA (FIPS~204) is the NIST-standardised lattice-based digital signature, designed for a single signer. Splitting its
key among several parties is hard because rejection sampling and rounding do not combine well with secret sharing.
NIST's 2026 First Call for multi-party threshold schemes (IR~8214C) has four proposals whose signatures verify under an
unmodified ML-DSA verifier: Mithril, Quorus, SplitForge/Trilithium and TALUS. We survey twenty-four sources on these
schemes and on leakage attacks against lattice signatures, and make the TALUS per-key signing cap our core problem. A
leakage-testing harness built in Phase~2 (ML-DSA passing 81 NIST ACVP vectors, scheme views, least-squares,
bounded-noise and integer-programming estimators) recovers the TALUS $s_2$ channel secret of one ML-DSA-44 ring element
from about $7\times10^4$ signatures ($2^{16.1}$), which is 3.1 bits above the cap and 0.9 bits above the authors'
uniqueness wall in our channel model: a thinner margin, not a break. Controls give 9/9 recoveries on a leaky variant and
0/18 false keys on genuine ML-DSA. Along the way we reproduce both published attacks on an early TALUS version and find a
parameter-proof gap in Mithril and a one-signature key exposure in a predecessor design; the latter two await
coordinated disclosure.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\textbf{Digital signatures and the quantum threat.} A digital signature lets a signer prove that a message came from them
and was not altered. RSA and ECDSA would be broken by a large quantum computer, so NIST standardised post-quantum
replacements. ML-DSA, specified in FIPS~204~\cite{fips204} and derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium~\cite{dilithium}, is the
primary choice; its security rests on the lattice problems Module-LWE (learning with errors) and Module-SIS.

\textbf{How ML-DSA signs.} Intuitively, the signer hides its secret inside a random-looking answer and throws the answer
away whenever it might give the secret away. Formally, all arithmetic is in the ring $R_q=\mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^n+1)$:
polynomials of degree below $n=256$ with coefficients modulo the prime $q=8380417$. $A$ is a public $k\times\ell$ matrix of
such polynomials. The secret key is a pair of short vectors $(s_1,s_2)$ with coefficients in $[-\eta,\eta]$, and the
public key is $t=As_1+s_2$, of which only the high part $t_1$ is published ($t=t_12^d+t_0$ with $d=13$ dropped bits
$t_0$). To sign, the signer samples a random nonce $y$ with coefficients below $\gamma_1$, computes $w=Ay$ and its high
bits $w_1=\HB(w)$, derives a challenge $c=H(\mathit{msg},w_1)$, a polynomial with exactly $\tau$ coefficients equal to
$\pm1$ and the rest 0, and outputs $z=y+cs_1$ with a short hint $h$. Here $\norm{x}$ is the largest absolute coefficient
of $x$, and $\beta=\tau\eta$ bounds $\norm{cs}$ for any secret $s$. Two rejection checks are applied:
$\norm{z}<\gamma_1-\beta$, which hides $s_1$, and $\norm{\LB(w-cs_2)}<\gamma_2-\beta$, which hides $s_2$ ($\gamma_2$ is
the rounding range of $\LB$). If either fails the signer restarts with a fresh $y$. This ``Fiat--Shamir with aborts''
structure makes accepted signatures statistically independent of the key. Table~\ref{tab:params} lists the parameter
values.

\textbf{Why rejection makes signatures safe.} Picture $z$ as a point in a box. Because $y$ is uniform on a box of
half-width $\gamma_1$ and $cs_1$ is a small shift of at most $\beta$, every point inside the smaller box of half-width
$\gamma_1-\beta$ is equally likely whichever $s_1$ was used; accepting only those points therefore hides $s_1$ completely.
Because $c$ has only $\tau$ nonzero entries, anything that does leak gives the attacker one noisy linear equation with
small coefficients per signature, which underlies all the sample counts below. Any threshold design that changes what is
accepted, what is rejected or what else is published must re-establish this independence, and this is the property we
test.

\textbf{Why thresholding is hard.} In a $(T,N)$ threshold scheme any $T$ of $N$ parties can sign and fewer than $T$ learn
nothing. Shamir secret sharing~\cite{shamir} hides a secret as the constant term of a random degree-$(T-1)$ polynomial;
Feldman~\cite{feldman} adds commitments $g^{a_i}$ to its coefficients; threshold Schnorr schemes such as
FROST~\cite{frost} simply add partial signatures. ML-DSA breaks all three conveniences: shares of a short secret are not
short; $\HB$ is not additive, so parties cannot round their own pieces of $w$; the $s_2$ check needs the full secret; and
the lattice analogue of a Feldman commitment, $Ax$, is not hiding (Section~\ref{sec:attacks}). Two facts drive most attacks
below. $A$ is tall ($k\ge\ell$), so publishing $Ax$ reveals $x$ by Gaussian elimination. And $z=y+cs_1$ holds over the
integers, so any leak of the nonce $y$ gives $s_1=c^{-1}(z-y)$.

\textbf{Why now, and what this report contributes.} NIST's First Call (IR~8214C)~\cite{ir8214c} expects preliminary
packages from late 2026 and full packages not before March 2027, so scrutiny is most useful now. Following the Phase~1
review, the core problem is the TALUS signing cap (P1); the Mithril, rejected-attempt and Kao questions (P2--P4) are
stretch goals. We give (i) a survey of the four proposals and the attack toolbox, (ii) the problem formulation, (iii) a
working leakage-testing harness (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}) and (iv) its Phase~2 results with trial counts and seeds.

\textbf{Survey method.} Sources came from the First Call submissions page, the proposals' ePrint/arXiv entries and their
predecessors, and searches of ePrint, TCHES, EUROCRYPT and ASIACRYPT (``Dilithium rejected signatures ILP'', ``ILWE'',
``threshold lattice signatures''). A source was kept if it defines, attacks or supplies a technique against a threshold
ML-DSA design. Metadata of all references were verified online (Shamir~\cite{shamir} and Feldman~\cite{feldman} against
Crossref).

\textbf{Scope and honesty note.} Quorus~\cite{quorus} and Trilithium~\cite{trilithium} were read in full;
Mithril~\cite{mithril} in full except Appendices D, E and most of G; Kao~\cite{kao} main text and Appendices B--O. Our own
harness code was run (Section~\ref{sec:solution}); the schemes' reference code was not, except the Mithril authors'
parameter script. Every TALUS number relies on our channel model, because the v0.22 writeup does not list the values
released per signature. Results are labelled as measured, derived or hypothesis.

\section{Literature Survey}

\subsection{Background}
Dilithium~\cite{dilithium} introduced the signing structure above and FIPS~204~\cite{fips204} standardised it. Early
lattice threshold signatures used threshold homomorphic encryption (G\"ur, Katz and Silde~\cite{gks}); Threshold
Raccoon~\cite{raccoon} uses only symmetric primitives and one-time additive masks but does not output ML-DSA signatures;
del Pino and Niot~\cite{finally} give a compact Raccoon-based scheme limited to $2^{64}$ signatures and about 8 parties. An
earlier preprint on identifiable aborts~\cite{dpia} is withdrawn and listed for history only. The decisive shift is toward
output that verifies under the unmodified FIPS~204 verifier, because deployed certificates and pinned keys cannot change.

\textbf{Rounding and the public value $v$.} A hint lets the verifier recompute $w_1$ from $v=Az-ct_12^d$. Substituting
$z=y+cs_1$ shows $v=w-c(s_2-t_0)$, so $v$ is public and carries the term $cs'$ with $s'=s_2-t_0$. The second rejection
check keeps $\LB(w)$ at least $\beta$ away from a rounding boundary, so $v$ reveals nothing; once the check is removed or
altered, that protection disappears.

\subsection{The four First-Call ML-DSA proposals}

\textbf{Mithril}~\cite{mithril,mithrilposter,mithrilpw} (PQShield, Brave, Bristol; USENIX Security 2026) uses replicated
secret sharing with short shares and local rejection with hyperball (Euclidean-ball) sampling, about 1\,MB per participant
for up to six parties, with a Go implementation. Security is a game-based reduction to MLWE and ML-DSA unforgeability under
static corruption of up to $T-1$ parties, without identifiable aborts. Each attempt succeeds with probability about $1/2$
(ML-DSA: about $1/4$), with $K$ parallel repetitions, and each party publishes a full MLWE-sample commitment, so the
bare-$Ax$ attack does not apply. The proof uses R\'enyi divergence and covers $Q_s=2^{50}$ signing queries. The superseded
version~\cite{reloaded} is not relied on. The preview writeup~\cite{mithrilpw} adds that up to 8 parties remain practical,
that no synchronised broadcast channel is needed, and that the Go code uses floating point for hyperball sampling.

\textbf{Why local rejection is delicate.} Single-signer ML-DSA accepts a nonce only if both checks pass, about $1/4$ of
the time. With $N$ parties each holding a short additive share, the sum of their responses must land in the same box, but
no party knows the sum. Mithril lets each party sample its nonce share from a hyperball, so the aggregate has a
predictable norm, and decides acceptance locally. The accepted responses are then close to, but not exactly, the FIPS~204
distribution; how close is a quantitative question (problem P2), not a bug.

\textbf{Quorus}~\cite{quorus} (J.P.\ Morgan; USENIX Security 2026) modifies signing to be MPC-friendly while keeping
FIPS~204 verification: the nonce is uniform, a small noise term $e_w$ is added to $w$, and $w_1$ is output even when
$(z,h)$ is rejected. It tolerates fewer than $n/2$ corruptions, is proven in the universal composability (UC) framework,
and costs about 150\,KB per party per attempt (0.31--0.59\,MB per signature, 16 or 29 rounds), benchmarked up to 63
parties. Always releasing $w_1$ lets the Fiat--Shamir hash be computed in the clear; the proof that rejected partial
signatures can be simulated relies on the noise $e_w$.

\textbf{SplitForge/Trilithium}~\cite{trilithium} (Cybernetica) targets a server and a phone plus a correlated randomness
provider (CRP). It is UC-secure against one malicious party among the three, takes 14 rounds per signing attempt, and
publishes the high bits $w_H$ even for rejected attempts, justified by an MLWR-type assumption that the paper itself calls
non-standard for ML-DSA parameters. It uses an actively secure comparison protocol and a new rounding protocol, has a Rust
implementation, is given both for unmodified ML-DSA and for a Quorus-style variant with CRP-supplied noise $e_w$, and is
part of Cybernetica's SplitKey submission. The writeup concedes that a malicious CRP can mount selective-disclosure attacks
that the authors consider harmless for the intended use.

\textbf{TALUS}~\cite{talus,taluspw} (Codebat) introduces the Boundary Clearance Condition (BCC): for a constant fraction of
nonces (43.2\%, 31.7\% and 39.1\% at ML-DSA-44/65/87) $s_2$ provably cannot push $w$ across a rounding boundary, so
rejection can be done offline on preprocessed nonces, giving a two-round distributed MPC profile. The paper states that
its cap-based shield for the $s_2$ channel is model-supported rather than proven, and proves that any FIPS-exact scheme
revealing a summed (Irwin--Hall) nonce admits key recovery after about $2^{30}$ signatures. A TEE-assisted one-round
variant appears in v0.22 only as a contrasting example. TALUS builds on Kao's Shamir nonce DKG~\cite{kao}, where the
signing nonce is itself Shamir-shared with pairwise-cancelling PRF masks and claimed nonce-share min-entropy above
$5\times$ the key entropy for signing sets up to 17.

\subsection{Published attacks on TALUS}\label{sec:attacks}
Niot~\cite{niot} (ePrint 2026/1386) gives two practical attacks on earlier TALUS versions. \textbf{Attack~1} exploits
non-hiding commitments: TALUS-MPC broadcast $As_{1,i}$ at key generation and $A\hat y$ in the blame phase. $A$ is
left-invertible except with probability at most $2^{-15}$ (ML-DSA-44) or $2^{-38}$ (-65/-87), so $x=A^{+}(Ax)$ reveals
every key share and the nonce, and $s_1=c^{-1}(z-y)$ follows from one signature. \textbf{Attack~2} targets the removed
$s_2$ check. Intuitively, every signature reveals a very noisy linear equation in the secret, and enough of them average
the noise away. Precisely, with $\HB(w)$ known from the hint, the observable is $b=-cs'+e$ with $e=\LB(w)$ roughly uniform
on $[-\gamma_2,\gamma_2]$. This is integer LWE (ILWE, LWE without reduction mod $q$), which least squares
solves~\cite{ilwe}: the per-coefficient error is about $\sigma_e/\sqrt{N\tau}$ with $\sigma_e^2\approx\gamma_2^2/3$, and
rounding succeeds once this is below $1/2$, so
\begin{equation}
N \approx \frac{4\gamma_2^2}{3\tau}\label{eq:ilwe}
\end{equation}
signatures, about $2^{28}$--$2^{31}$ (Table~\ref{tab:ilwe}). Niot stresses that (\ref{eq:ilwe}) is unoptimised, and that
reinstating the check is not enough because a corrupted party can bias its nonce share (e.g.\ $y_i=0$) so the check always
passes.

The authors' response (v0.22~\cite{taluspw}) replaces commitments by zero-knowledge well-formedness proofs and handles the
$s_2$ channel with a mandatory per-key signing cap of about $2^{13}/2^{14}/2^{14}$ (ML-DSA-44/65/87), enforced by key
rotation, against a claimed integer-uniqueness wall near $2^{15.2}/2^{16.4}/2^{16.2}$ below which the observations cannot
determine $s_2$ by themselves. Each preprocessed nonce is bound to one quorum and erased after use. The cap is the entire
defence of the $s_2$ channel, which makes it the natural core target; it also hurts deployability, because certificates
and pinned keys cannot rotate every few thousand signatures.

\textbf{Worked illustration of Attack~1.} Working in the NTT domain, $A$ splits into 256 independent $k\times\ell$
matrices over $\mathbb{Z}_q$, one per slot. Each is left-invertible unless an event of probability about $1/q$ occurs,
which is the origin of the $2^{-15}$ and $2^{-38}$ bounds, so any observer solves the system slot by slot and obtains
$s_{1,i}$ exactly; shortness is irrelevant because no lattice problem arises. With $k=\ell=1$ the commitment is just
$a\cdot s\bmod q$, and dividing by $a$ recovers $s$. The reasoning ``discrete-log commitments hide, so lattice ones
should'' fails because $g^x$ hides $x$ only since discrete logarithms are hard, whereas $Ax$ is a linear map and linear
maps are easy to undo.

\textbf{Worked illustration of Attack~2.} Consider one coefficient of $s'$. A single sample $b=-cs'+e$ constrains a sum of
$\tau$ secret coefficients to a window about $2\gamma_2$ wide, far wider than the secret's range, so it says almost
nothing. Averaging over independent challenges shrinks the uncertainty like $1/\sqrt{N\tau}$, and once it falls below
$1/2$ each coefficient can be rounded. The factor $\gamma_2^2/\tau$ in (\ref{eq:ilwe}) shows why ML-DSA-44, with the small
$\gamma_2=(q-1)/88$, is the most exposed per signature. Two points matter for P1: (\ref{eq:ilwe}) treats the noise as
Gaussian, wasting the information in its sharp edges, and it recovers $s'$ one coefficient at a time, whereas ILP and
lattice reduction use all constraints jointly.

\subsection{Leakage-based cryptanalysis of lattice signatures}\label{sec:toolbox}
Bootle et al.~\cite{ilwe} introduced the ILWE view of linear leakage in BLISS. Zhou et al.~\cite{rejected} show that the
challenges of rejected Dilithium signatures bound $cs$ from both sides and recover keys by integer linear programming
(ILP). Damm et al.~\cite{concealed} define Concealed ILWE, where only a fraction of samples leak, and break masked
Dilithium with Huber and Cauchy regression. Yates et al.~\cite{yates} find least-squares attacks on captured signatures
insufficient at the parameters they studied. Hence any threshold design that releases values correlated with $cs_1$ or
$cs_2$ must be tested with estimators stronger than least squares: bounded-noise estimators, ILP, robust regression and
lattice reduction.

\textbf{Why a summed nonce leaks.} If each of $T$ parties adds a uniform nonce share, the aggregate follows a bell-shaped
Irwin--Hall law, and $z=y+cs_1$ is that bell shifted by $cs_1$, so each signature carries a little Fisher information about
$s_1$. Kao~\cite{kao} bounds the per-signature loss below 0.013 bits, but~\cite{talus} proves an attack after about
$2^{30}$ signatures at ML-DSA-65; a design must make the revealed nonce exactly uniform (the claim of Quorus and
Trilithium) or cap the number of signatures (TALUS).

\textbf{Corruption models.} Static corruption fixes the corrupted set before the protocol starts; adaptive corruption
chooses it during execution. Dishonest-majority security (Mithril) allows $T-1$ corrupted signers; honest-majority
security (Quorus, TALUS-MPC) requires $N\ge2T-1$. Without identifiable aborts, as in Mithril, a corrupted party can abort
after seeing honest contributions and retry, so aborted transcripts join the adversary's view. The harness therefore
treats the corruption model as an input, not a constant.

\textbf{Countermeasures.} Masking splits secrets into shares so that no intermediate value correlates with the key,
but~\cite{concealed} shows masking alone fails when a fraction of samples leak with low noise. Against rejection leakage
the defence is to hide which check failed and to release nothing derived from a rejected response, which is why Quorus's
release of $(w_1,c,\bot)$ needs its careful simulation argument. Costs differ: masking costs time, a signing cap costs key
rotation, and hiding rejected transcripts costs interaction.

\subsection{Related and superseded work}
The Mithril line began as a poster~\cite{mithrilposter} and a withdrawn preliminary ePrint~\cite{reloaded}; all Mithril
claims here come from~\cite{mithril,mithrilpw}. Kao's design~\cite{kao} is not a Call submission, but TALUS builds on it
and it shows the same patterns. Its fully distributed profile P2 broadcasts masked commitments, and the paper's own remarks
show that an exposed $\lambda_hAy_h$ yields $y_h$ and then the key; the masks hide these only when $|S\setminus C|\ge2$,
that is, when at least two signers are honest. The paper concedes that at $T=N$ with $N-1$ corruptions mask hiding fails
in P2, yet its Table~1 still claims dishonest-majority unforgeability with UC proofs against static adversaries. Its
Irwin--Hall analysis bounds the loss by 0.013 bits per signing query, stated as non-vacuous for $q_s<16{,}000$.

\subsection{Gap analysis and comparison}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No systematic public testing} of Mithril, Quorus or Trilithium transcripts, only design-level arguments.
\item \textbf{TALUS's cap} sits about 2 bits below the authors' wall and 14--17 bits below Niot's unoptimised estimate
(\ref{eq:ilwe}); an optimised bounded-noise or ILP estimator could close this gap.
\item \textbf{Rejected-attempt transcripts} (Quorus, Trilithium) and \textbf{Mithril's accepted distribution and hint loss}
(7--12 bits reported; proof for $Q_s=2^{50}$ only) lack independent re-derivation.
\item \textbf{Corruption-model gaps}: static-only proofs, honest-majority boundaries and side conditions such as
$|S\setminus C|\ge2$.
\end{itemize}
Mithril alone tolerates a dishonest majority but is limited to $N\le6$; Quorus scales but needs an honest majority and many
rounds; Trilithium covers phone-and-server with a trusted helper; TALUS minimises rounds but its $s_2$ protection is a usage
cap rather than a structural argument. Independent testing therefore adds most value where a proof contains a margin
(TALUS), a heuristic (Trilithium) or a changed distribution (Mithril). Evidence quality differs as well: Mithril and Quorus
have reductions in the (quantum) random oracle model, Trilithium a UC proof resting on an MLWR-type assumption, and TALUS
an information-theoretic argument with a numerical margin. A margin is the one kind of evidence that can be checked
directly by experiment, which is a further reason to make the TALUS cap the core problem.

\subsection{Literature matrix}
Table~\ref{tab:matrix} rates each surveyed source for relevance (H central, M supporting, L background) and marks its
themes: TS threshold signatures, MD ML-DSA compatible, AT attack or leakage, SS secret sharing or MPC, RS rejection
sampling. Of the 24 sources, 13 are central, 6 supporting and 5 background; sixteen appeared in 2025 or 2026, and 22 of 24
in 2018 or later. The sources marked AT are the evidence base for the estimators in Section~\ref{sec:solution}.
Ref.~\cite{devevey}, added in Phase~2 for the Mithril finding, is not part of the survey matrix.

\textbf{Design rules that follow from the survey.} Do not publish any linear image of a secret or nonce under $A$, however
it is masked, unless the mask stays hiding under the worst-case corruption allowed (checklist items 1 and 8). Treat the
nonce as secret as the key, including in blame and abort procedures (item 2). Keep every single-signer rejection check or
prove that the replacement leaks no more (item 3). And bound the signatures per key only with a margin that an optimised
estimator, not just least squares, cannot close (item 4). The First-Call history shows these rules are easy to violate
when importing discrete-log patterns.

\subsection{Attack surface: Phase 1 hypotheses and verdicts after full reading}\label{sec:surface}
Each Phase~1 hypothesis is listed with its verdict after reading the papers and running the harness (details in

\textbf{Quorus.} (i) Reveal-on-reject is proven simulatable: confirmed, but the proof is computational (MLWE), not

\textbf{TALUS v0.22.} The cap is the whole $s_2$ argument and the noise has sharp edges: measured in
Section~\ref{sec:p1}. \textbf{Kao P2:} hypothesis strengthened to a key exposure (Section~\ref{sec:kao}).

\section{Problem Formulation}

\subsection{Threat model and notation}

sees while $m$ is signed, including broadcasts, corrupted parties' state and aborted attempts. The adversary statically

\subsection{Core problem P1: the TALUS signing cap}
\textbf{Intuition.} TALUS removes the $s_2$ check, so each signature leaks one noisy equation in $s'$, and the defence is
to stop before enough equations accumulate. Because BCC confines the noise $e$ to $(-\gamma_2+\beta,\gamma_2-\beta)$, the
noise has hard edges: a single sample near an edge excludes many candidate secrets, so a bounded-noise estimator can need
far fewer samples than least squares, whose error falls only like $1/\sqrt N$. \textbf{Statement.} Given observations
$b_j=-c_js'+e_j$ with $e_j$ confined to the BCC interval, find the number of signatures $N^*$ at which $s'$ is recovered
(and checked against the public key) with probability 0.99, for each parameter set, and compare $\log_2N^*$ with the cap
and the wall. A value below the cap is a break; above it, $\log_2N^*-\log_2\text{cap}$ is the margin in bits.
\textbf{Success criterion:} $N^*$ with stated trials and seeds, a positive control (estimator recovers a leaky variant) and
a negative control (no false keys on genuine ML-DSA).

\textbf{Why the cap needs measuring.} Because the public key already determines $s_2$, protection below the wall is
computational. The authors model a syndrome meet-in-the-middle at cost $2^{H/2}$ with residual entropy $H\ge502$ (about
$2^{160}$), but the only proven floor is about $2^{58}$ at ML-DSA-65 and $2^{49}$ at -87 (below 128 bits), and ML-DSA-44 has
no public cokernel ($k=\ell$). Above the wall the observations alone determine $s'$, so an efficient estimator that
succeeds there recovers the key with no lattice work; between cap and wall, safety rests on that unproven model. Hence P1
measures $N^*$ directly. \textbf{Hypothesis (stated in Phase~1):} bounded-noise and ILP estimators beat the least-squares
estimate (\ref{eq:ilwe}) by a wide margin.

\subsection{Stretch goals P2--P4}
\textbf{P2 (Mithril).} Check the $Q_s=2^{50}$ accounting and the a posteriori key-sharing hint loss for the exact share
subset of $T-1$ corrupted parties. \textbf{P3 (rejected attempts).} Test whether rejected-attempt transcripts of Quorus and
Trilithium depend on $s_1$ or $s_2$. \textbf{P4 (Kao P2).} Decide whether the masked commitments hide $y$ when
$|S\setminus C|=1$, as the paper's Table~1 implies. Phase~1 hypotheses were: P2, a wall above $2^{64}$ (a null result)
with the hint loss re-derived; P3, Quorus simulatable thanks to $e_w$ while Trilithium's assumption holds only
approximately; P4, failure at $|S\setminus C|=1$. Success criteria were a numerical bound with derivation (P2), a
statistical test with stated false-positive rate and sample budget (P3), and an explicit recovery on a toy instance or a
reason why the masks hide (P4).

\section{The Leakage-Testing Harness}\label{sec:solution}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.68\columnwidth]{harness_architecture.png}
\caption{Harness architecture. Our ML-DSA core produces every signing attempt with its secret internals; a scheme view
keeps only what that scheme releases (secrets stop there); an adapter turns the observations into pairs $(c,b)$; the
estimators never see the secret, and the experiments score them against ground truth.}\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Design (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}).} (1)~\textbf{ML-DSA core}: NTT, SHAKE sampling, hints and sign/verify that keeps every
attempt; it matches the dilithium-py implementation byte for byte and passes 81 official NIST ACVP vectors (key
generation, signing, verification) for all three parameter sets. (2)~\textbf{Scheme views}: plain ML-DSA (negative
control), a $y$-leaking variant (positive control), TALUS-BCC, TALUS v0.1 and Mithril, plus stand-alone Quorus, Trilithium
and Kao simulators. (3)~\textbf{Estimators}: least squares; a bounded-noise ``edge'' estimator, posed as a Chebyshev linear
program (minimise the largest residual $|b+cs'|$) and solved on CPU or GPU; an ILP finishing step that searches for an
integer $s'$ consistent with every bound; and theory curves (Fisher information and Cram\'er--Rao bounds). Every recovery
is checked against ground truth, and every run is seeded and logged.

\textbf{Estimators, with intuition.} Least squares averages: stacking $N$ samples $(c,b)$ gives an overdetermined integer
system whose solution has per-coefficient error variance about $\sigma_e^2/(N\tau)$, so error falls like $1/\sqrt N$.
Bounded noise uses the edges: if every $|e|$ is at most a known bound, each sample is a pair of linear inequalities
$|b+cs'|\le$ bound, and the feasible region (a polytope) shrinks roughly like $1/N$ rather than $1/\sqrt N$; the Chebyshev
LP finds its centre and rounding gives $s'$. ILP finishing replaces that rounding by an integer search inside the
polytope, in the manner of~\cite{rejected}, which helps when a few coefficients sit near a rounding boundary. Fisher
information $I$ per signature sets the Cram\'er--Rao bound on any unbiased estimator's variance; key recovery then needs
on the order of $4/(I\tau)$ signatures~\cite{talus}. Robust regression (Huber, Cauchy~\cite{concealed}) and
lattice-estimator hint accounting are kept for schemes where only a fraction of samples leak or where corrupted parties
hold partial information.

\textbf{A view in action (TALUS).} For each signing attempt the core records $y$, $w$, $c$, $z$ and which check failed.
The TALUS view (1) keeps only attempts whose nonce satisfies BCC, as the offline filter would; (2) drops the secret
fields; (3) emits what an observer can compute, $v=Az-ct_12^d$ and the hint, together with $c$; the adapter then forms
$b=-cs'+e$. A corrupted-coalition variant would add the corrupted parties' shares, and for schemes with aborts the view
emits exactly the fields the specification says are revealed for a rejected attempt, no more. Because the estimator
receives only the view's output, any key it recovers was recoverable from public data.

\textbf{Implementation.} Python with numpy and scipy; the linear programs use the HiGHS solver; the ring products inside
the edge estimator, about 90\% of its run time, run on an NVIDIA GPU with PyTorch in double precision, about $3\times$
faster than the CPU version and checked against it. Least squares is solved exactly in one pass: after an FFT the
equations for each frequency are independent, so each frequency is a single division (agreeing with an iterative solver
to $10^{-13}$). Transcripts for several trials are built in parallel processes while the GPU solves.

\textbf{Evaluation and validity.} Every experiment reports signatures used, recovered fraction, cost and parameter set.
$N^*$ is the smallest $N$ with success $\ge0.99$ over the stated trials (interpolated between grid points). Recovery is
verified against ground truth, so partial correlations are never counted as breaks. Internal threats (primitive bugs,
estimator over-fitting) are addressed by the ACVP vectors and by many random keys; external threats (our simulator
differing from the real protocol, parameters changing before the packages) remain and are why every TALUS number is
labelled as model-based; a negative result is phrased only as a bound on what was tried.

\textbf{End-to-end check (gate B).} Real ML-DSA-44 attempts passed through the TALUS view and the unchanged estimator
recovered $s'$ for 2/2 keys at $N=2\times10^5$ and $4\times10^5$ accepted signatures and 0/2 at $10^5$ (seed 2029),
consistent with $N^*$ in Table~\ref{tab:margin}.

\textbf{Least-squares calibration (Fig.~\ref{fig:ls}).} To validate the simulator and the error law behind (\ref{eq:ilwe})
we measured the least-squares error on ML-DSA-44, $n=256$ (one ring element), plain $\LB$ noise,
$N\in\{10^3,3\cdot10^3,10^4,3\cdot10^4,10^5,3\cdot10^5\}$, 10 trials per $N$, seed 2033 (generator
\texttt{default\_rng([2033,N,trial])}). The mean RMS error stays within 2\% of $\gamma_2/\sqrt{3\tau N}$ at every
$N$. Real TALUS v0.1 signatures (keys from seeds 101--103) fall on the same curve. Extrapolating, rounding every
coefficient needs on the order of $10^9$ signatures, so least squares alone is far from the cap; the bounded-noise
estimator is what matters for P1.

For ML-DSA-44 the full secret (all $k=4$ elements) was recovered in 20/20 trials at $1.46\times10^5$. With the ILP

it returned the key for 0/18 keys (15 of them from seed 2027), only the public-equivalent $-t_0$, so the false-positive
rate is below about 15\% at 95\% confidence. \textbf{Verdict:} no break; the margin above the cap is 3--4.5 bits and above

\textbf{From toy rings to full size.} We first ran the edge estimator on reduced rings ($n=8$ to 128, 10--20 trials per
point, ML-DSA-44 numbers). Per-element $N^*$ flattened around $1$--$2\times10^5$ ($n=64$: $2.8\times10^5$; $n=128$:

the harder target $s_2-t_0$ needed the same $N^*$ as $s_2$, and plain $\LB$ noise behaved like BCC noise. Relative to plain
least squares (about $3.6\times10^9$ in our model) the edge estimator is worth about four orders of magnitude, yet it stops
3--4.5 bits short of the cap. The theory module explains the shape: for one coefficient alone the uniform-noise error falls
like $1/N$ exactly (checked by Monte Carlo), and when all 256 coefficients are solved jointly a fitted coupling constant
(about $0.76\,\tau^{0.42}$) accounts for the real $N^*$ sitting 1.5--2 bits above the single-coefficient formula. ILP
finishing lowers the 50\% point by $1.0\times$, $1.1\times$, $1.1\times$ and $1.5\times$ at $n=8$, 16, 32 and 64, consistent
with the roughly $2\times$ at $n=256$.

\textbf{Limits of the P1 result.} (i) The channel model (independent uniform noise on the BCC interval, no wrap-around, no
use of the hint) is our reading of TALUS, not quoted from the specification. (ii) The ILP figure is per ring element with 8
trials per point. (iii) Lattice reduction and better use of hints were not tried, so $N^*$ is an upper bound on what an
attacker needs. (iv) Our wall and cap figures are the authors'; we did not re-derive the wall.

\subsection{TALUS v0.1 and Mithril}\label{sec:mithril}
\textbf{TALUS v0.1 (Niot's attacks reproduced).} On our implementation of the arXiv v2 design~\cite{talus} (ML-DSA-44,
$|S|=3$, keys from seeds 101--103; DKG simplified to Shamir sharing), attack~1 recovered $s_1$ exactly from the
key-generation broadcast (3/3 keys) and from one MPC signature (60/60). Attack~2 by least squares follows the theory curve
up to $N=10^5$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ls}) and would need about $1.2\times10^9$ signatures. A separate sanity run on real no-check
ML-DSA-44 transcripts gave $0.97\times$ Niot's error formula (12 runs), and on the same transcripts the edge estimator
needed about $1{,}500\times$ fewer signatures than his least-squares $N$. Simplifications: plain ML-DSA key generation
followed by Shamir sharing of $s_1$ instead of the paper's DKG, the MPC computation of $w_1$ replaced by its correct output,
and $|S|=T=3$ throughout. The exercise shows why TALUS changed its design, and that our views reproduce a published break
exactly.

\textbf{Mithril proof-parameter gap (P2).} Theorem~3.2 of~\cite{mithril} bounds the number of safe queries through a
hyperball-intersection lemma taken from the ePrint full version of Devevey et al.~\cite{devevey} (App.~A.6). That bound
uses exponent $n-1$ where a correct cylinder argument gives $(n-1)/2$; for ML-DSA-44 it gives $2^{-50.1}$ where the exact
value is $2^{-33.5}$ (reproduced with the authors' own Sage parameter script). With the published parameters the theorem
then covers $Q_s=2^{26}$--$2^{37}$ rather than $2^{50}$. Under the typical norm distribution (heuristic, not a proof) most
sets still reach $2^{50}$, some fall up to about 3 bits short; corrected parameters need $1.3\times$ to $8.5\times$ more
parallel repetitions $K$. The faulty bound is ePrint-only (the ASIACRYPT version states only the exact formula), and among
13 citing works we checked only Mithril uses it; HAETAE, the other main hyperball scheme, evaluates the exact
incomplete-Beta value numerically and is unaffected. Seen from the other side, reaching $2^{50}$ with the published radii
would require the norm bound $B$ in the proof to sit only 0--7 standard deviations above the mean norm of $cs$, whereas the
proof needs an overwhelming bound (the paper's footnote says about 13). This is a proof gap, not an attack: exceeding the
bound slightly enlarges a R\'enyi-divergence budget and does not obviously reveal $s$. \textbf{Hint loss.} With the exact
share subset of $T-1$ parties, the lattice estimator gives losses of 4.9--7.7 (ML-DSA-44), 17.7--23.9 (-65) and 8.6--14.4
bits (-87), against the reported 7--12 (Table~\ref{tab:hint}).

\subsection{Kao's P2 profile (P4)}\label{sec:kao}
The paper's own text (Fig.~1b, Remark~11, App.~M.1) has every party broadcast $W_i$, and its pairwise masks cancel
(Lemma~1), so $\sum_i W_i=Ay$ is public; Gaussian elimination gives $y$ and then $s_1=c^{-1}(z-y)$ from a single P2
signature, for any $|S\setminus C|$. The proof of Corollary~5 itself calls this value the public broadcast, and no later
appendix changes it. Confirmed by algebra and a toy implementation; the author's code is not public. The toy (ring size
8, real $q$) also checks the narrower Phase~1 hypothesis: with all but one signer corrupted, the honest party's mask can be
stripped and $\lambda_hAy_h$ inverted, while with two honest signers this route fails, as the paper says; the sum route
above, however, works whatever the number of honest parties. Two further points: Corollary~6 claims the full
threshold-signature functionality without the $|H|\ge2$ condition that its Simulator~17 needs, and the key-generation
commitment $t^{(i)}=As_1^{(i)}+s_2^{(i)}$ carries the noise $s_2^{(i)}$, so the bare $As_{1,i}$ pattern broken in TALUS
does not recur there.

\subsection{Quorus and Trilithium (P3)}\label{sec:p3}
Our Quorus implementation produced 0 of 272{,}664 signatures failing FIPS~204 verification. No statistical leak is
possible by construction at the level of single coefficients; a lattice-level distinguisher was not run. For Trilithium,
exact enumeration at $n=4$ and Monte Carlo at $n=8$ ($2\times10^8$ samples $\times$ 3 trials) show the gap between rejected
$w_H$ and the simulator shrinking with $\gamma_1$ and $n$ (branch~2: $3\times10^{-5}$ to $7\times10^{-7}$ at $n=8$). Paper
errata, not attacks: Quorus Functionality~6 uses the bound $\gamma_2-\beta-\eta$ where every other place uses
$\gamma_2-\beta$ (PDF p.~32), and Lemma~8 has a wrong normalising constant; Trilithium's simulation of unsuccessful signing
(p.~24) leaves the gap noted in Section~\ref{sec:surface}.

\textbf{Trilithium in more detail.} The released mixture after a rejection comes from two branches: branch~3 (the $z$
check failed) and branch~2 ($z$ passed but the $\LB$ check failed). Exact enumeration at $n=4$ showed a real but small gap
between rejected $w_H$ and the simulator, shrinking with $\gamma_1$ (branch~3 roughly as $\gamma_1^{-2.7}$). A branch-2
floor near $10^{-4}$ persisted for $\gamma_1\ge\gamma_2$ at $q=257$, 513 and 1025, so it is not a small-$q$ artefact; it
comes from the wrap-around rounding cell, and masking that cell removes it (30--65$\times$ smaller). Exact convolution for
$n=2$ to 6 at $q=17$ shows both gaps falling with $n$ (branch~3 by 2--3 orders of magnitude from $n=4$ to 6). At $n=8$ the
branch-3 gap is below the Monte Carlo floor of about $10^{-7}$. Toy sizes cannot prove anything about $n=256$, but every
trend points toward the assumption holding statistically; a lattice-level distinguisher remains untested. On H-F3, a
server that tampers with the rejection check is detected and stopped, so the leak is bounded by the stopping time, about
$\log_2(\text{attempts})$ bits, once; the issue is that the paper's simulation does not reproduce how that stopping
probability depends on the real $z$.

\textbf{Quorus in more detail.} Besides the two text errors, the batched-OR protocol has statistical soundness $2^{-39}$
($\kappa=40$), so about one rejected attempt in $2^{39}$ is wrongly declared a pass and its failing, key-dependent $z$ is
opened; at roughly $10^{11}$ signatures this is not practical, but 40-bit statistical security is lower than usual. A
rough reduction (ours, not run) suggests the MLWE instance behind Theorem~2 is about as hard as ML-DSA key recovery itself.

stated here neutrally as our reading of public documents. Quorus and Trilithium items are errata.

\section{Phase 3 Prototype and Conclusion}
\textbf{What the Phase 3 prototype will demonstrate.} An end-to-end TALUS-cap margin tool: given transcripts produced by
the TALUS reference code (packages expected from March 2027) through a scheme view, it runs the bounded-noise estimator with
ILP finishing, reports $N^*$ and the margin in bits against the cap and the wall, and runs the positive and negative
controls automatically, so a reported margin is never quoted without them. Concretely it will (i) replace our channel
model by the released values of the real code, (ii) extend ILP finishing to the full secret and to ML-DSA-65/87, and (iii)
rerun the Mithril parameter check on the submitted parameters. Success means reproducing Table~\ref{tab:margin} on real
transcripts, or explaining any difference, with each margin reported together with its controls. Until packages appear,
the prototype runs on our TALUS view, so the demonstration does not depend on the release date. The demonstration will be
a single command that takes a transcript file and a parameter set and prints the margin table with its controls, trial
counts and seeds; the stretch goals continue alongside it (a lattice-level distinguisher for rejected transcripts, the Go
signing code of Mithril, and disclosure to the Kao and Mithril authors).

\textbf{Research questions, answered.} (RQ1) Which threshold ML-DSA designs exist? Four First-Call proposals, differing in
corruption model, party count and rounds (Table~\ref{tab:schemes}). (RQ2) Which are broken? Earlier TALUS versions, by two
attacks that we reproduced; Kao's P2 profile exposes the key from one signature by the paper's own text. (RQ3) Which
techniques work? Linear inversion of published $Ax$, nonce-leak recovery, and for the TALUS channel a bounded-noise
estimator with ILP finishing, about four orders of magnitude better than least squares. (RQ4) Where is the evidence
thinnest? The TALUS cap margin (now measured at 3--4.5 bits above the cap), Mithril's parameter-proof accounting, and the
untested lattice-level question for rejected transcripts in Quorus and Trilithium.

\textbf{Lessons from Phase~2.} Three methodological points carry over to Phase~3. First, controls change conclusions:
without the negative control the estimator's output $-t_0$ on genuine ML-DSA could have been mistaken for leakage. Second,
reduced-size runs can mislead in either direction (the ML-DSA-87 shortcut at $n=64$ overstated the margin, and ILP gains
grow with $n$), so only full-size numbers are quoted. Third, reading the papers in full revised two of our own Phase~1
statements (Trilithium releases $w_H$, not a partial signature; uniformity holds per coefficient only), which is why every
hypothesis in Section~\ref{sec:surface} now carries a verdict.

\textbf{Positioning.} Earlier cryptanalysis of lattice signatures~\cite{ilwe,rejected,concealed,yates} targets a single
signer leaking through a physical side channel. Here the leakage is logical: it is part of the protocol transcript, visible
to any observer or corrupted party, and its noise distribution is known exactly from the specification. Results therefore
transfer across implementations, and schemes analysed only by proof gain an independent empirical check.

\textbf{Ethics.} We study only public, standardisation-stage specifications, as NIST invites. Findings about a scheme are
shared with its authors before any public posting, and we distinguish confirmed key recovery, measured margins, proof gaps
and errata, labelling results that rely on our simulators rather than the authors' code.

\textbf{Conclusion.} The survey of twenty-four sources showed that threshold ML-DSA designs fail when patterns from
discrete-log protocols are imported, and that three of the four proposals had not been tested. Phase~2 built and validated
a harness and measured the core quantity: the TALUS cap holds in our model, but by 3--4.5 bits above the cap and only about
1--2 bits above the authors' wall, much less than least squares suggests. Secondary findings are a proof-parameter gap in
Mithril, a key exposure in Kao's P2 profile, and errata in Quorus and Trilithium, with disclosure to the authors to follow
before any public posting.
