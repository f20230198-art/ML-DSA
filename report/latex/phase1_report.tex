\begin{abstract}
ML-DSA (FIPS~204) is the NIST-standardised lattice-based digital signature, but it was designed for a single signer.
Splitting the signing key among several parties, so that no single device holds the key, is hard because ML-DSA's
rejection sampling and rounding steps do not combine well with secret sharing. In 2026 NIST opened a First Call for
multi-party threshold schemes (IR~8214C), and four proposals aim to produce signatures that verify under an unmodified
ML-DSA verifier: Mithril, Quorus, SplitForge/Trilithium and TALUS. This report surveys twenty-four papers and
specifications covering these schemes, their predecessors, and the cryptanalytic tools (integer learning with errors,
integer linear programming, robust regression) used to attack lattice signatures through leakage. The survey shows that
earlier versions of TALUS fall to two practical key-recovery attacks, while no public attack on the other three exists,
largely because they have not been systematically tested. We formulate four concrete open problems and propose a
reusable leakage-testing harness that simulates each scheme's public transcripts, including aborted attempts, and runs
a battery of statistical key-recovery tests against them.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
ML-DSA, FIPS 204, threshold signatures, lattice cryptography, cryptanalysis, integer LWE, rejection sampling.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

\textbf{Digital signatures and the quantum threat.} A digital signature lets a signer prove that a message came from them
and was not altered. Today's signatures (RSA, ECDSA) would be broken by a large quantum computer, so NIST has
standardised post-quantum replacements. ML-DSA, specified in FIPS~204~\cite{fips204} and derived from
CRYSTALS-Dilithium~\cite{dilithium}, is the primary general-purpose choice. Its security rests on the lattice problems
Module-LWE (learning with errors) and Module-SIS.

\textbf{How ML-DSA signs.} Let $A$ be a public $k\times\ell$ matrix over the ring $R_q=\mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^{256}+1)$ with
$q=8380417$, and let the secret key be short vectors $(s_1,s_2)$ with public key $t=As_1+s_2$ (low bits dropped). To sign,
the signer samples a random short nonce $y$, computes $w=Ay$ and $w_1=\HB(w)$, derives a sparse challenge
$c=H(\mathit{msg},w_1)$, and outputs $z=y+cs_1$ with a hint $h$. Two rejection checks are applied:
$\norm{z}<\gamma_1-\beta$, which hides $s_1$, and $\norm{\LB(w-cs_2)}<\gamma_2-\beta$, which hides $s_2$ and keeps the hint
small. If either fails, the signer restarts with a fresh nonce. This ``Fiat--Shamir with aborts'' structure makes
signatures statistically independent of the key: inside the acceptance box, $z$ is uniform whichever $s_1$ was used.

\textbf{Parameters.} All three parameter sets share $q=8380417$, $n=256$ and $d=13$ dropped bits of $t$. They differ as in
Table~\ref{tab:params}, where $\tau$ is the number of $\pm1$ entries of $c$ and $\beta=\tau\eta$ bounds $\norm{cs}$.

\textbf{Threshold signatures.} In a $(T,N)$ threshold scheme any $T$ of $N$ parties can sign together, while fewer than
$T$ learn nothing about the key. Shamir secret sharing~\cite{shamir} hides a secret as the constant term of a random
degree-$(T-1)$ polynomial; Feldman's verifiable secret sharing~\cite{feldman} adds public commitments $g^{a_i}$ so each
party can check its share. Threshold Schnorr schemes such as FROST~\cite{frost} exploit linearity: partial signatures
simply add. ML-DSA breaks each of these conveniences. Shares of a short secret are not short, which violates the norm
bounds that rejection sampling relies on; $\HB$ is not additive, so parties cannot round their own pieces of $w$; the
$s_2$ check needs the full secret; and, as Section~\ref{sec:attacks} shows, the lattice analogue of a Feldman commitment
($A\cdot x$) is not hiding at all.

\textbf{Why cryptanalysis now.} NIST's First Call (IR~8214C)~\cite{ir8214c} invites threshold schemes, with preliminary
packages from late 2026 and a package deadline not before March 2027. Public scrutiny is most valuable before schemes
are frozen. Two facts make ML-DSA-based designs fragile. First, $A$ is tall ($k\ge\ell$ in every set), so for any short
$x$, publishing $Ax$ reveals $x$ by Gaussian elimination; no lattice problem is involved. Second, $z=y+cs_1$ holds over
the integers, so any leak of the nonce $y$ gives $s_1=c^{-1}(z-y)$.

\textbf{Contributions.} (i) A structured survey of the threshold ML-DSA landscape and the attack toolbox; (ii) a gap
analysis; (iii) four formal open problems; (iv) a proposed solution, a leakage-testing harness, with a first experiment.

\textbf{Survey methodology.} Sources were collected in three passes: the NIST First Call submissions page (the four
proposals and their preview writeups); each proposal's ePrint/arXiv entry and its predecessors; and searches for
``Dilithium rejected signatures ILP'', ``ILWE'' and ``threshold lattice signatures'' on ePrint, TCHES, EUROCRYPT and
ASIACRYPT. A source was included if it defines a threshold ML-DSA construction, attacks one, or supplies a technique used
by such an attack. Bibliographic data of 22 of the 24 references were verified online; the two classical references
(Shamir, Feldman) are cited from memory. The selection is weighted to 2024--2026 because the area barely existed before.

\textbf{Scope note.} This is a literature-and-specification assessment. No scheme's reference code was run; the only
experiment is a small simulation of the least-squares estimator on synthetic TALUS-style data
(Section~\ref{sec:prelim}). Statements about attack surfaces are labelled as hypotheses.

\section{Literature Survey}

\subsection{Background: lattice threshold signatures}
Dilithium~\cite{dilithium} introduced the signing structure above, and FIPS~204~\cite{fips204} standardised it as ML-DSA.
Early lattice threshold signatures used heavy tools: G\"ur, Katz and Silde~\cite{gks} give a two-round scheme from
threshold homomorphic encryption. Threshold Raccoon~\cite{raccoon} (EUROCRYPT 2024) uses only symmetric primitives and
simple lattice operations, with one-time additive masks that stop partial keys leaking through partial signatures, but
its signatures are not ML-DSA signatures. Del Pino and Niot~\cite{finally} give a compact Raccoon-based threshold
signature limited to $2^{64}$ signatures and about 8 parties, and del Pino et al.~\cite{dpia} add identifiable aborts
(since withdrawn in favour of a later paper). The decisive recent shift is toward schemes whose output verifies under the
\emph{unmodified} FIPS~204 verifier, because deployed certificates, hardware and pinned keys cannot change.

\textbf{Rounding functions that matter for attacks.} ML-DSA compresses $t$ by Power2Round, $t=t_1 2^d+t_0$, and publishes
only $t_1$. During signing, Decompose splits $w$ into $w_1$ and a low part bounded by $\gamma_2$. The verifier computes
$v=Az-ct_1 2^d=w-c(s_2-t_0)$, so $v$ is public and carries the term $c(s_2-t_0)$. The second rejection check keeps
$\LB(w)$ at least $\beta$ from a rounding boundary, which makes this leaked noise uninformative; when the check is removed
or altered, that protection disappears.

\subsection{The four First-Call ML-DSA proposals}

\textbf{Mithril}~\cite{mithril,reloaded,mithrilposter,mithrilpw} (PQShield, Brave, Bristol) is the first practical
ML-DSA-compatible threshold scheme. It uses \emph{replicated} secret sharing with short shares, so shares stay within
ML-DSA's norm bounds, and performs rejection sampling locally per party with hyperball sampling, avoiding a global abort
MPC. Communication is about 1\,MB per party for up to six parties, with a Go implementation. Security is a game-based
reduction to MLWE and ML-DSA unforgeability under static corruption of up to $T-1$ parties. There are no identifiable
aborts; the per-attempt success probability is $1/2$ instead of ML-DSA's roughly $1/4$, so the accepted-response
distribution differs from FIPS~204. Each party publishes a full MLWE-sample commitment (so the bare-$Ax$ attack does not
apply). The proof uses R\'enyi divergence and covers only $Q_s=2^{50}$ signing queries. The paper appears at USENIX
Security 2026.

\textbf{Quorus}~\cite{quorus} (J.P.\ Morgan) modifies ML-DSA signing to be MPC-friendly while keeping FIPS~204
verification and signature size. It targets honest majority ($<n/2$ corruptions), benchmarks up to 63 parties, needs
about 150\,KB online per party per attempt (0.31--0.59\,MB per signature, 16 or 29 rounds) and is proven in the universal
composability (UC) framework. Its variant draws the nonce uniformly, adds a small noise term $e_w$ to $w$, and always
outputs $w_1$ even when $(z,h)$ is rejected; the proof argues that rejected partial signatures are simulatable.

\textbf{SplitForge/Trilithium}~\cite{trilithium} (Cybernetica) targets two parties, a server and a phone, plus a
correlated-randomness provider (CRP). It is UC-secure against one malicious party, uses an actively secure comparison
protocol and a new rounding protocol, and has a Rust implementation (3 rounds for keygen, 14 per signing attempt). It
publishes the high bits $w_H$ even for rejected attempts; its argument for this rests on an MLWR-type assumption that the
paper itself calls non-standard for ML-DSA parameters.

\textbf{TALUS}~\cite{talus,taluspw} (Codebat) introduces the \emph{Boundary Clearance Condition} (BCC): for a constant
fraction of nonces (31.7\% at ML-DSA-65) $s_2$ provably cannot push $w$ across a rounding boundary, so rejection checks
can be done offline on preprocessed nonces. This yields a distributed MPC profile with two online rounds. The paper
also proves a lower bound: any FIPS-exact scheme that reveals a summed (Irwin--Hall) nonce admits key recovery after
about $2^{30}$ signatures. TALUS builds on Kao's Shamir nonce DKG~\cite{kao}, where the nonce itself is Shamir-shared
with pairwise-cancelling PRF masks.

\subsection{Published attacks on TALUS}\label{sec:attacks}
Niot~\cite{niot} (ePrint 2026/1386) gives two independent practical attacks on earlier TALUS versions.

\textbf{Attack 1: non-hiding commitments.} TALUS-MPC used Feldman-style commitments $A\cdot x$ copied from discrete-log
protocols. Since $A$ is left-invertible except with probability at most $2^{-15}$ (ML-DSA-44) or $2^{-38}$ (-65/-87),
$x=A^{+}(Ax)$. The key-generation broadcast $As_{1,i}$ reveals every key share, and the blame phase's $A\hat y$ reveals the
nonce, giving $s_1=c^{-1}(z-y)$ from \emph{one} signature. In the NTT domain $A$ splits into 256 independent $k\times\ell$
matrices over $\mathbb{Z}_q$; each is solved slot by slot, and the shortness of the secret is irrelevant.

\textbf{Attack 2: the removed $s_2$ check.} Each signature releases $v=w-c(s_2-t_0)$; with $\HB(w)$ known from the hint,
the observable is $b=-cs'+e$ with $s'=s_2-t_0$ and $e=\LB(w)$ roughly uniform. This is ILWE (LWE without modular
reduction), which Bootle et al.~\cite{ilwe} showed is solvable by least squares. The estimated requirement is
\begin{equation}
N \gtrsim \frac{4\gamma_2^2}{3\tau}\label{eq:ilwe}
\end{equation}
signatures, i.e.\ about $3.1\times10^8$ (ML-DSA-44), $1.9\times10^9$ (-65) and $1.5\times10^9$ (-87). Niot stresses these
figures are unoptimised. Reinstating the check alone is not enough, because a corrupted party can bias its nonce share
(e.g.\ $y_i=0$) so the check always passes.

The authors' response (preview writeup v0.22~\cite{taluspw}) replaces commitments by zero-knowledge well-formedness
proofs and handles the $s_2$ channel by a mandatory per-key signing cap of about $2^{13}$/$2^{14}$/$2^{14}$ signatures,
enforced by key rotation, against a claimed integer-uniqueness wall near $2^{15.2}$/$2^{16.4}$/$2^{16.2}$. Whether this
margin is real is exactly the kind of question this project targets; the cap also hurts deployability, since
certificates and pinned keys cannot rotate every few thousand signatures.

\textbf{Why a summed nonce leaks.} In many designs each of $T$ parties adds a uniform nonce share. The sum is not uniform
but bell-shaped (Irwin--Hall), so $z=y+cs_1$ is a bell shifted by $cs_1$, and each signature leaks a little Fisher
information about $s_1$. The loss per signature is tiny (below 0.013 bits in~\cite{kao}), but it accumulates, and
\cite{talus} proves an efficient attack after about $2^{30}$ signatures at ML-DSA-65. A design avoids this only by making
the revealed nonce exactly uniform or by limiting the number of signatures.

\subsection{Leakage-based cryptanalysis of lattice signatures}
The tools behind these attacks come from work on ILWE and rejection-sampling leakage. Bootle et al.~\cite{ilwe}
introduced the ILWE view of linear leakage in BLISS. Zhou et al.~\cite{rejected} (TCHES 2025) show that the challenges of
\emph{rejected} Dilithium signatures leak: rejected responses impose bounds on $cs$, which they turn into an integer
linear program (ILP), recovering full keys in seconds to minutes, validated on an ARM Cortex-M4. Damm et
al.~\cite{concealed} (ASIACRYPT 2025) define Concealed ILWE, where only a fraction of samples is informative, show that
ordinary least squares fails, and use Huber and Cauchy robust regression to break masked Dilithium in under two minutes.
Yates et al.~\cite{yates} test least-squares attacks on ILWE built from captured signatures and find that, at their
parameters, the schemes' claimed security holds. Together these show that any threshold design releasing values
correlated with $cs_1$ or $cs_2$ must be examined with estimators stronger than plain least squares.

\textbf{Countermeasures.} Masking and shuffling defend against side channels, but~\cite{concealed} shows masking alone
fails when a fraction of samples leak. Against rejection leakage, the defence is to hide which check failed and release
nothing derived from a rejected response, which is why Quorus's choice to release $(w_1,c,\bot)$ needs a careful
simulation argument. These defences cost time (masking), key rotation (caps) or interaction (hiding rejected
transcripts).

\subsection{Comparative discussion}
The proposals sit at different points of a trade-off between corruption tolerance, number of parties, interaction and
strength of evidence. Mithril alone tolerates a dishonest majority but is limited to $N\le6$. Quorus scales to many
parties with UC proofs but needs an honest majority and many rounds. Trilithium covers the phone-and-server case with an
extra trusted randomness provider. TALUS minimises rounds, but its security is least settled: the $s_2$ channel is closed
by a usage cap, not a structural argument. Evidence quality differs too: Mithril and Quorus have reductions, Trilithium a
UC proof with a heuristic, TALUS an information-theoretic argument with a numerical margin. Independent empirical testing
adds most value where the proof contains a heuristic or margin (Trilithium, TALUS) and where parameters change the
accepted distribution (Mithril).

\subsection{Gap analysis}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No systematic public testing} of Mithril, Quorus or Trilithium transcripts, only design-level arguments.
\item \textbf{TALUS's cap} sits about 2 bits below the authors' wall and 14--17 bits below Niot's unoptimised estimate; an
optimised bounded-noise or ILP estimator could close this gap.
\item \textbf{Rejected-attempt transcripts} (Quorus reveal-on-reject, Trilithium's MLWR-type assumption) have no
empirical leakage analysis in the literature we found.
\item \textbf{Mithril's accepted-response distribution} and its key-sharing hint loss (7--12 bits reported) lack
independent re-derivation; the proof covers $2^{50}$ queries, below the usual $2^{64}$.
\item \textbf{Corruption-model gaps}: static-only proofs, honest-majority boundaries ($N=2T-1$) and side conditions such
as $|S\setminus C|\ge2$ in Kao's profile P2~\cite{kao}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem Formulation}

\subsection{Threat model and notation}
Fix an ML-DSA parameter set $(q,k,\ell,\tau,\gamma_1,\gamma_2,\beta)$. A $(T,N)$ threshold scheme $\Pi$ has a transcript
distribution $\mathrm{Tr}_\Pi(\mathit{sk},m)$ over everything an adversary observes while signing $m$: the final
signature, broadcasts, coordinator messages, the internal state of corrupted parties and, crucially, the data of
\emph{aborted} attempts. The adversary statically corrupts up to the number of parties the scheme allows, observes $q_s$
signing sessions on chosen messages, and wins if it recovers $s_1$ (equivalently the secret key). We study key recovery,
which is stronger evidence of breakage than a distinguishing bias. Notation is summarised in Table~\ref{tab:notation}.

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Notation}\label{tab:notation}
\centering\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\columnwidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
Symbol & Meaning\\
\midrule
$A,t$ & Public $k\times\ell$ matrix over $R_q$; public key $t=As_1+s_2$\\
$s_1,s_2$ & Short secrets; $s'=s_2-t_0$, $t_0$ the dropped low bits of $t$\\
$y,z,c$ & Nonce, response $z=y+cs_1$, sparse challenge ($\tau$ entries $\pm1$)\\
$w,w_1,e$ & $w=Ay$, $w_1=\HB(w)$, rounding residue $e=\LB(w)$\\
$(T,N)$ & Threshold and parties; $S$ signing set, $C$ corrupted set\\
$q_s$ & Number of signing sessions observed\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\subsection{Problem statement}
\emph{Given the public specification of each ML-DSA-compatible First-Call threshold scheme, determine whether the
transcripts it publishes (including aborted attempts and the view of a corrupted coalition) carry enough information
about the secret key to allow key recovery with a feasible number of signatures, and if not, measure the margin in
signatures and bits of security.} We split this into four open problems.

\textbf{P1 (TALUS signing cap).} Below an integer-uniqueness wall $q_\mathrm{uniq}\approx2^{15.2}/2^{16.4}/2^{16.2}$ the
noisy observations $O=r_0+c(t_0-s_2)$ do not by themselves determine $s_2$, and the cap $2^{13}$--$2^{14}$ sits about two
bits below it. Since the public key already determines $s_2$, protection is computational: the authors model a
meet-in-the-middle cost of $2^{H/2}$ with residual entropy $H\ge502$, but the only proven floor is about $2^{58}/2^{49}$
at ML-DSA-65/87 (below 128 bits), and ML-DSA-44 has no public cokernel ($k=\ell$). P1 is to measure the true cost of
recovering $s'$ from at most $2^{14}$ sharp-edged observations, using bounded-noise estimators, ILP and hint-aware lattice
estimators.

\textbf{P2 (Mithril accepted-response leakage).} Compute the Fisher information $I$ about $s_1$ carried by one accepted
response under Mithril's hyperball sampling and $1/2$ acceptance, derive the attack wall $\approx4/(I\tau)$ signatures,
and compare it with the proven $Q_s=2^{50}$ and the usual $2^{64}$. Also re-derive the hardness loss of the
a-posteriori key-sharing hint for the exact share subset held by $T-1$ corrupted parties.

\textbf{P3 (Rejected-attempt leakage).} Quorus and Trilithium release $w_H$ and $c$ for rejected attempts, and Mithril
releases each party's commitment even when its partial response is rejected. Quorus adds noise $e_w$ to make this
provably simulatable, while Trilithium keeps unmodified ML-DSA and relies on an MLWR-type assumption. Test whether
rejected-attempt transcripts, with the accept/reject bit, depend on $s_1$ or $s_2$, and quantify the bits per attempt that
Trilithium's declassified rejection bit reveals about $cs_2$.

\textbf{P4 (Kao's profile P2).} Paper~\cite{kao} states that in profile P2 the broadcast commitments are hidden only when
$|S\setminus C|\ge2$, and its own remarks show that an exposed $\lambda_h A y_h$ yields $y_h$ and then
$s_{1,h}=c^{-1}(z_h-y_h)$. With $|S|=T$ and $T-1$ corruptions, $|S\setminus C|=1$, yet the paper claims dishonest-majority
unforgeability. Decide whether P2 is secure only for $|S|\ge T+1$.

\subsection{Hypotheses and success criteria}
For P1 we hypothesise that hint-aware estimators beat the least-squares estimate by a wide margin; the criterion is a
measured cost to recover $s'$ at $2^{14}$ observations, verified against the public key at reduced parameters. For P2 we
expect the wall to exceed $2^{64}$, so a numerical bound with its derivation is the criterion. For P3 we expect Quorus to
be simulatable as claimed while Trilithium's assumption may hold only approximately; the criterion is a statistical test
with a stated false-positive rate and sample budget. For P4 we expect failure at $|S\setminus C|=1$; the criterion is an
explicit recovery on a toy instance or a precise reason why the masks still hide.

\section{Possible Solution: A Leakage-Testing Harness}

\subsection{Architecture}
We propose a reusable software harness with three layers.
(1)~\textbf{ML-DSA primitives}: parameters, NTT over $R_q$, Power2Round, Decompose, MakeHint/UseHint and the FIPS~204
samplers, validated against known-answer tests.
(2)~\textbf{Scheme simulators}: each emits the public transcript on synthetic keys, covering accepted and aborted
attempts and the view of a corrupted coalition.
(3)~\textbf{Estimators}, keyed to the attack checklist of Table~\ref{tab:checklist}: Gaussian elimination for published
$Ax$; direct nonce-leak recovery; least squares/ILWE regression; bounded-noise and ILP estimators; robust (Huber/Cauchy)
regression~\cite{concealed}; Fisher-information computation; and lattice-estimator accounting for hints. Estimators take
a stream of transcript records and return a candidate secret and a confidence score, so a new scheme needs only a new
simulator. Keys are synthetic, so ground truth is always available, and every claimed recovery is checked by
recomputing $t$ from the candidate $s_1$.

\caption{Attack checklist distilled from the survey}\label{tab:checklist}
\centering\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\columnwidth}{@{}lXX@{}}
\toprule

\midrule

2 & Does any nonce leak? & $s_1=c^{-1}(z-y)$\\
3 & Are rejection checks removed or altered? & ILWE regression on $cs$\\
4 & Is accepted $z$ non-uniform? & Fisher information wall\\
5 & What do rejected attempts reveal? & ILP on rejection bounds~\cite{rejected}\\

\subsection{Estimators in more detail}
\textbf{Least squares.} Each signature gives $(c,b)$ with $b=-cs'+e$. Stacking $N$ samples gives an overdetermined integer
system whose least-squares error per coefficient has variance about $\sigma_e^2/(N\tau)$. For uniform noise on
$[-\gamma_2,\gamma_2]$, $\sigma_e^2\approx\gamma_2^2/3$, and rounding succeeds once $\sigma_e/\sqrt{N\tau}<1/2$, giving
(\ref{eq:ilwe}).

\textbf{Bounded-noise estimation.} Under BCC the noise lies in a known interval, so each sample is a pair of linear
inequalities that rules out every candidate with $|b+cs'|$ above the bound. The feasible region shrinks faster than the
least-squares confidence ellipsoid. We implement this as an ILP in the manner of~\cite{rejected} and as a cheaper
iterated-elimination heuristic.

\textbf{Robust regression.} When only a fraction of samples are informative, Huber and Cauchy losses downweight outliers
and succeed where least squares fails~\cite{concealed}.

\textbf{Fisher information.} For accepted responses with density depending on $s$ through a shift $cs$, the Fisher
information $I$ per signature gives the Cram\'er--Rao bound; key recovery needs on the order of $4/(I\tau)$
signatures~\cite{talus}. We compute $I$ numerically from each scheme's exact acceptance region.

\textbf{Hint accounting.} Partial information (e.g.\ Mithril's share subsets) is modelled as extra linear equations and
fed to a lattice estimator to measure the bit loss.

\subsection{Method}
Step 1 reproduces Niot's ILWE attack~\cite{niot} on TALUS-style transcripts at reduced parameters and checks
(\ref{eq:ilwe}). Step 2 (P1) swaps in bounded-noise/ILP estimators and plots recovery probability against sample count.
Steps 3--4 (P2, P3) run the same estimators on Mithril, Quorus and Trilithium simulators, with a null result stated as
``no detectable dependence up to a stated sample budget''. Step 5 (P4) is direct algebra on the published protocol.
When full packages appear (from March 2027) the harness is rerun on reference code.

Every experiment reports the signatures consumed, the recovered key fraction, wall-clock cost and the parameter set.
Validation proceeds in stages: primitives must pass FIPS~204 known-answer tests; each simulator must be broken in its
deliberately leaky variant (e.g.\ publishing $Ay$) to confirm the estimators have power; and null results are reported
with the sample budget and the attack classes tried.

\subsection{Preliminary result}\label{sec:prelim}
Table~\ref{tab:ilwe} evaluates (\ref{eq:ilwe}); these $2^{28}$--$2^{31}$ signatures are $2^{12}$--$2^{15}$ times the
TALUS cap. A first harness (18 unit tests) simulates $b=-cs+e$, $e=\LB(w)$, for ML-DSA-44 and recovers $s$ by least
squares. The measured error matches $\gamma_2/\sqrt{3\tau N}$ within 3\% for $N=5{,}000$ to $160{,}000$. Extrapolating,
rounding all 1{,}024 coefficients of $s_2$ needs about $3.6\times10^9$ signatures (50\% success), roughly 12 times
(\ref{eq:ilwe}). The model ignores $t_0$, bounded noise and lattice reduction, so the formula should be read as an order
of magnitude.

\textbf{Feasibility.} Each problem reduces to a bounded computation: P1 is linear algebra on simulated pairs, P2 a
numerical integral plus an estimator call, P3 a two-sample test, P4 algebra on a toy instance. None needs proprietary
code or solving hard lattice problems at full security, because the attacks exploit protocol leakage rather than
breaking MLWE.

\textbf{Ethics.} Targets are public standardisation-stage specifications, as NIST invites. Any finding will be shared
with the scheme teams before public posting, and results will distinguish confirmed breaks, margins and null results,
labelling clearly which ones rely on our simulators rather than the authors' code.

\section{Conclusion}
Threshold ML-DSA is moving from research to standardisation, and the published break of early TALUS versions shows how
easily thresholding can leak a key through commitments or removed rejection checks. Our survey of twenty-four sources
finds three schemes without public attacks mainly because they are untested, one scheme whose security now rests on a
signing cap with an uncertain margin, and a toolbox (ILWE, ILP, robust regression) mature enough to test them. We
formulated four open problems and proposed a leakage-testing harness, with a first experiment confirming the
least-squares error law. Phase 2 will complete the harness, reproduce the TALUS attack, and run the estimators on the
remaining schemes.
